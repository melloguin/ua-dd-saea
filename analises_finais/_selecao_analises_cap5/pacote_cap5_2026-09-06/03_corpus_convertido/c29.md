## A statistical model-based algorithm for ‘black-box’ multi-objective optimisation 

Antanas Zilinskas<sup>*</sup> 

Institute of Mathematics and Informatics, Vilnius University, Akademijos str. 4, LT-08663 Vilnius, Lithuania 

(Received 27 November 2010; final version received 7 January 2012) 

The problem of multi-objective optimisation with ‘expensive’ ‘black-box’ objective functions is considered. An algorithm is proposed that generalises the single objective P-algorithm constructed using the statistical model of multimodal functions and concepts of the theory of rational decisions under uncertainty. Computational examples are included demonstrating that the algorithm proposed possess several expected properties. 

Keywords: multi-objective optimisation; global optimisation; statistical models; Pareto optimal set 

### 1. Introduction 

Uncertainty is an important circumstance that complicates the solution of many real-world optimisation problems. Even in the cases where a single objective function and constraints are defined by analytical formulas, unimodality of the considered problem frequently cannot be proven. In such cases there is no guarantee that the solution found by the standard mathematical programming software is the global optimum indeed. The uncertainty is especially challenging in cases of ‘black-box’ optimisation of ‘expensive’ functions where multimodality, nondifferentiability and imprecision in computations cannot be excluded; moreover, the maximum allowable number of trials (computations of values of the objective function) is strongly restricted. 

To cope with such challenges in the single objective optimisation case, the statistical models of multimodal functions have been proven very helpful, as shown by Mockus (1988), Strongin and Sergeyev (2000) and To¨ rn and Zilinskas (1989). A priori uncertainty about potential objective functions is modelled by a stochastic function (frequently called a stochastic process for scalar argument and a random field for vector argument). It is assumed that the considered objective function is a random realisation of the stochastic function. Information acquired during the search, namely, the function values at the points selected in the previous iterations, is used for planning of further iterations via a conditional distribution of values of the stochastic function. The site for the current computation of the objective function value is chosen by maximising the expected improvement defined using 

ideas of the utility theory comprehensively presented, e.g. by Fishburn (1970). 

In this article, a single objective global optimisation algorithm, based on a statistical model of multimodal functions, namely the P-algorithm, is generalised for the case of ‘black-box’ multi-objective optimisation. For the axiomatic definition of statistical models of multimodal ‘black-box’ functions and the P-algorithm, we refer to Zilinskas (1982, 1985).

However, there remains a class of important problems without sufficient attention of researchers: namely, the problems with ‘black-box’, multimodal and ‘expensive’ objectives. The objective functions are supposed to be ‘expensive’ if computation of the (vector) value of F(X) is time consuming. 

Multiobjective optimisation problems with ‘expensive’ objective functions are typical in engineering design, where time consuming computations are involved for modelling technological processes. Frequently the available software implements only an algorithm to compute the values of objective functions, but neither details of implementation nor analytical properties of the objective functions are known, although the continuity can be normally assumed. The ‘black-box’ global optimisation of ‘expensive’ functions is a difficult problem even in the single objective case. In the deterministic approach, further assumptions are normally made that enable the construction of underestimates for function values, e.g. Lipschitz underestimates discussed by Horst, Pardalos, and Thoai (2007). To the best knowledge of the author, these algorithms have not been generalised for a multiobjective case. Another approach is based on statistical models of objective functions validated by Zilinskas (1982). This approach includes the P-algorithm, Bayesian, information theory-based, and kriging methods (see Zilinskas (1985), Mockus (1988), Strongin and Sergeyev (2000) and Huang, Allen, Notz, and Zheng (2006) correspondingly). Some algorithms for the single objective optimisation of ‘expensive’ functions are developed using surrogate functions, e.g. radial basis functions used in Gutmann (2001). Those methods are also called response surface-based methods (see, e.g. Jones 2001). It should be noted that despite very different assumptions, radial basis global optimisation methods are equivalent to the P-algorithm for the corresponding statistical model, as shown by Gutmann (2001) and Zilinskas (2010). Since the statistical models and the utility theory-based global optimisation is a well theoretically justified approach to the single objective global optimisation of ‘expensive’ functions, it seems worth generalising to the multi-objective case. In this article, we consider the generalisation of the P-algorithm to the multi-objective case. 

### 3. Statistical model

based on statistical models. In this article, we assume that the objective functions considered are random realisations of the stochastic function, chosen for a model. However, the algorithm considered below can also be interpreted in terms of more general statistical models constructed by Zilinskas (1982), using the ideas of subjective probabilities, that are discussed from all angles by Fishburn (1986) and Savage (1954). ### 5. Methodology of a preliminary experimental assessment 

Theoretical and algorithmic achievements of multiobjective optimisation have also implied expanding applications. Expensive multimodal black-box problems are a relatively little researched subfield in multiobjective optimisation. However, it is important for applications, and deserves more attention from researchers. Since the single objective P-algorithm well corresponds to the challenges of global optimisation of expensive black box functions, it seemed reasonable to be generalised to the multi-objective case. As shown in the previous sections, the theoretical generalisation is rather straightforward. Some experimental investigation was planned to find out how much the properties of the multi-objective P-algorithm correspond to the expectations. 

General methodological concepts of testing and comparison of mathematical programming algorithms and software are well developed (McGeoch 2002). The methodology, called ‘competitive testing’ by 

Hooker (1995), should normally be applied in the comparison of the well-established algorithms. This methodology is also extended for testing and comparison of multi-objective optimisation algorithms (see, e.g. Fonseca and Fleming 1996; Miettinen 1999; Zitzler, Deb, and Thiele 2000; Zitzler, Thiele, Laumanns, Fonseca, and Fonseca 2003; Deb 2009). In the case of the well-researched classes of problems (e.g. convex multi-objective optimisation), this methodology is universally applicable, only the selection of test functions should be specially selected taking into account the properties of the considered sub-class of problems, e.g. considered by Gaviano, Kvasov, Lera, and Sergeyev (2003) and Mathar and Zilinskas (1994). For example, the tests, based on special cases of realworld applied problems, can be very useful for evaluating the efficiency of the respective algorithms; see, e.g. Pardalos, Radziukyniene, and Zilinskas (2011) where multi-objective portfolio problems are used for testing the algorithms aimed to distribute solutions uniformly in the Pareto optimal set. 

However, for the experimental versions of algorithms, aimed at relatively little researched sub-classes of problems, the so-called ‘scientific testing’ seems most suitable as discussed by Hooker (1995). The main goal of the ‘scientific testing’ is to check whether the implementation of an algorithm possesses the expected properties. The suitability of the ‘scientific testing’ for the newly proposed multi-objective methods is advocated in Deb (2009), although the term ‘scientific testing’ is not used there. We cite Deb (2009, p. 320): ‘When a new and innovative methodology is initially discovered for solving a search and optimisation problem, a visual description is adequate to demonstrate the working of the proposed methodology. . . . it is important to establish, in the mind of the reader, a picture of the new suggested procedure’. 

The efficiency of the single objective P-algorithm is the result of a rational distribution of observation points: the density of the points is higher in promising subsets of the feasible region. Correspondingly, it can be expected that the multi-objective P-algorithm distributes the observation points more densely closer to the efficient points in the feasible decision region implying a denser distribution of the solutions close to the Pareto set. That property is considered heuristically by means of visualisation as well as by computing some relevant criteria. Bi-objective problems with one and two variables were chosen for the experiments to enable visual analysis of the results. 

Note that ‘competitive testing’ of algorithms for minimising expensive ‘black-box’ functions is challenging, since the termination conditions, usually defined by the convergence criteria in case, the computation of objective functions is not time consuming, are not

Usually the admissible number of computations of objective function values is much smaller than that needed to achieve the convergence criteria used, e.g. for optimisation of functions defined by analytical formulas. To define the testing conditions adequate to those real-world optimisations situations, the termination conditions should be defined by the agreement of researchers and practitioners. In the experiments, presented in this article, we have stopped the optimisation after computing 100 objective function values. 

Many optimisation algorithms, especially heuristic ones, depend on numerous parameters. The values of those parameters are tuned to achieve efficiency in some typical situations, most frequently, supposing rather a large number of computations of function values. However, the parameter values tuned to the latter case can be very different for the case relevant to the optimisation of expensive functions. Therefore, a further experimental investigation is needed to prepare welljustified conditions for ‘competitive testing’, oriented to compare the performance of the selected algorithms. In this article, the Monte Carlo search is used for the comparison, since it is parameter-free, and can be considered as defining the origin on the ‘efficiency axis’.

### 7. Experimental results 

The MATLAB implementation of the multi-objective P-algorithm was used for experimentation. The results of minimisation obtained by the Monte Carlo method are presented for the comparison. Minimisation was stopped after 100 computations of objective function values. 

The sites for the first 50 computations of F(X) were chosen by the P-algorithm randomly with a uniform distribution over the feasible region. That data were used to estimate the parameters of the statistical model as well as in planning of the next 50 observations according to (6). The maximisation of the improvement probability was performed by a simple version of multistart. The values of the improvement probability (9) were computed at 1000 points, generated randomly with uniform distribution over the feasible region. A local descent was performed from the best point using the codes from the MATLAB Optimization Toolbox.

The problem of multi-objective optimisation with ‘expensive’ ‘black-box’ objective functions is considered. An
algorithm is proposed that generalises the single objective P-algorithm constructed using the statistical model of
multimodal functions and concepts of the theory of rational decisions under uncertainty. Computational
examples are included demonstrating that the algorithm proposed possess several expected properties.

1. Introduction
Uncertainty is an important circumstance that com-
plicates the solution of many real-world optimisation
problems. Even in the cases where a single objective
function and constraints are defined by analytical
formulas, unimodality of the considered problem
frequently cannot be proven. In such cases there is
no guarantee that the solution found by the standard
mathematical programming software is the global
optimum
indeed.
The
uncertainty
is
especially
challenging in cases of ‘black-box’ optimisation of
‘expensive’
functions
where
multimodality,
non-
differentiability
and
imprecision
in
computations
cannot be excluded; moreover, the maximum allowable
number of trials (computations of values of the
objective function) is strongly restricted.
To cope with such challenges in the single objective
optimisation case, the statistical models of multimodal
functions have been proven very helpful, as shown by
Mockus (1988), Strongin and Sergeyev (2000) and
To¨ rn and Zˇ ilinskas (1989). A priori uncertainty about
potential objective functions is modelled by a stochas-
tic function (frequently called a stochastic process for
scalar argument and a random field for vector argu-
ment). It is assumed that the considered objective
function is a random realisation of the stochastic
function. Information acquired during the search,
namely, the function values at the points selected in
the previous iterations, is used for planning of further
iterations via a conditional distribution of values of the
stochastic function. The site for the current computa-
tion of the objective function value is chosen by
maximising the expected improvement defined using
ideas of the utility theory comprehensively presented,
e.g. by Fishburn (1970).
In this article, a single objective global optimisation
algorithm, based on a statistical model of multimodal
functions, namely the P-algorithm, is generalised for
the case of ‘black-box’ multi-objective optimisation.
For the axiomatic definition of statistical models of
multimodal ‘black-box’ functions and the P-algorithm,
we refer to Zˇ ilinskas (1982, 1985).

5. Methodology of a preliminary experimental
assessment
Theoretical and algorithmic achievements of multi-
objective optimisation have also implied expanding
applications. Expensive multimodal black-box prob-
lems are a relatively little researched subfield in multi-
objective optimisation. However, it is important for
applications,
and
deserves
more
attention
from
researchers. Since the single objective P-algorithm
well corresponds to the challenges of global optimisa-
tion of expensive black box functions, it seemed
reasonable to be generalised to the multi-objective
case. As shown in the previous sections, the theoretical
generalisation is rather straightforward. Some experi-
mental investigation was planned to find out how
much the properties of the multi-objective P-algorithm
correspond to the expectations.
General methodological concepts of testing and
comparison of mathematical programming algorithms
and software are well developed (McGeoch 2002).
The
methodology,
called
‘competitive
testing’
by
Hooker (1995), should normally be applied in the
comparison of the well-established algorithms. This
methodology is also extended for testing and compar-
ison of multi-objective optimisation algorithms (see,
e.g. Fonseca and Fleming 1996; Miettinen 1999;
Zitzler,
Deb,
and
Thiele
2000;
Zitzler,
Thiele,
Laumanns, Fonseca, and Fonseca 2003; Deb 2009).
In the case of the well-researched classes of problems
(e.g. convex multi-objective optimisation), this meth-
odology is universally applicable, only the selection of
test functions should be specially selected taking into
account the properties of the considered sub-class of
problems, e.g. considered by Gaviano, Kvasov, Lera,
and Sergeyev (2003) and Mathar and Zˇ ilinskas (1994).
For example, the tests, based on special cases of real-
world applied problems, can be very useful for
evaluating the efficiency of the respective algorithms;
see, e.g. Pardalos, Radziukyniene, and Zˇ ilinskas (2011)
where multi-objective portfolio problems are used for
testing the algorithms aimed to distribute solutions
uniformly in the Pareto optimal set.
However, for the experimental versions of algo-
rithms, aimed at relatively little researched sub-classes
of problems, the so-called ‘scientific testing’ seems
most suitable as discussed by Hooker (1995). The main
goal of the ‘scientific testing’ is to check whether the
implementation of an algorithm possesses the expected
properties. The suitability of the ‘scientific testing’ for
the newly proposed multi-objective methods is advo-
cated in Deb (2009), although the term ‘scientific
testing’ is not used there. We cite Deb (2009, p. 320):
‘When a new and innovative methodology is initially
discovered for solving a search and optimisation
problem, a visual description is adequate to demon-
strate the working of the proposed methodology. . . . it
is important to establish, in the mind of the reader, a
picture of the new suggested procedure’.
The efficiency of the single objective P-algorithm is
the result of a rational distribution of observation
points: the density of the points is higher in promising
subsets of the feasible region. Correspondingly, it can
be expected that the multi-objective P-algorithm dis-
tributes the observation points more densely closer to
the efficient points in the feasible decision region
implying a denser distribution of the solutions close to
the Pareto set. That property is considered heuristically
by means of visualisation as well as by computing some
relevant criteria. Bi-objective problems with one and
two variables were chosen for the experiments to
enable visual analysis of the results.
Note that ‘competitive testing’ of algorithms for
minimising expensive ‘black-box’ functions is challeng-
ing, since the termination conditions, usually defined
by the convergence criteria in case, the computation of
objective functions is not time consuming, are not

those parameters are tuned to achieve efficiency in some
typical situations, most frequently, supposing rather a

experimental investigation is needed to prepare well-
justified conditions for ‘competitive testing’, oriented to
compare the performance of the selected algorithms.

comparison, since it is parameter-free, and can be
considered as defining the origin on the ‘efficiency axis’.

8. Conclusions
The approach to a single objective global optimisation,
based on statistical models of uncertainty, and on the
axiomatic rational decision theory, is extended to
multi-objective optimisation. The constructed multi-
objective P-algorithm is aimed at the problems with
multimodal, ‘black box’, and ‘expensive’ objectives.
The multi-objective P-algorithm can be implemented
