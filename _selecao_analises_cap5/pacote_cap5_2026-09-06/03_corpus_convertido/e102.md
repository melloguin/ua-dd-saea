# Choose Appropriate Subproblems for Collaborative Modeling in Expensive Multiobjective Optimization 

Zhenkun Wang , _Member, IEEE_ , Qingfu Zhang, _Fellow, IEEE_ , Yew-Soon Ong , _Fellow, IEEE_ , Shunyu Yao, Haitao Liu , and Jianping Luo 

**_Abstract_ —In dealing with the expensive multiobjective optimization problem, some algorithms convert it into a number of single-objective subproblems for optimization. At each iteration, these algorithms conduct surrogate-assisted optimization on one or multiple subproblems. However, these subproblems may be unnecessary or resolved. Operating on such subproblems can cause server inefficiencies, especially in the case of expensive optimization. To overcome this shortcoming, we propose an adaptive subproblem selection (ASS) strategy to identify the most promising subproblems for further modeling. To better leverage the cross information between the subproblems, we use the collaborative multioutput Gaussian process surrogate to model them jointly. Moreover, the commonly used acquisition functions (also known as infill criteria) are investigated in this article. Our analysis reveals that these acquisition functions may cause severe imbalances between exploitation and exploration in multiobjective optimization scenarios. Consequently, we develop a new acquisition function, namely, adaptive lower confidence bound (ALCB), to cope with it. The experimental results on three different sets of benchmark problems indicate that our proposed algorithm is competitive. Beyond that, we also quantitatively validate the effectiveness of the ASS strategy, the CoMOGP model, and the ALCB acquisition function.**

## I. INTRODUCTION 

ANY real-world optimization problems involve more **M** than one objective [1]–[4]. Such problems are generally formulated as multiobjective optimization problems (MOPs). Moreover, if the objective function evaluation of candidate solutions is computationally or financially costly (e.g., it requires time-consuming simulations or expensive physical experiments), the problems are referred to as expensive MOPs [5]–[7]. Many surrogate-assisted optimization algorithms have been developed to cope with expensive MOPs [8]–[19]. Among them, some algorithms scalarize the MOP into a number of single-objective subproblems for optimization [20]–[24]. We refer to these algorithms as surrogate-assisted scalarization optimization algorithms (SASOAs), which are the focus of this article. 

The SASOA builds surrogate models (also known as metamodels) for the subproblems and uses the surrogate models instead of the original subproblems to evaluate solutions. Since the evaluation cost of the surrogate models is much cheaper than that of the subproblems, the SASOA can significantly reduce the computational and/or financial burden. In each iteration, the SASOA selects one or multiple subproblems to conduct surrogate-assisted optimization. However, these selected subproblems may be unnecessary or resolved (cf., Section III-A). Operating on such subproblems has low efficiency, especially in the scenario of expensive optimization. To overcome this drawback, we propose an adaptive subproblem selection (ASS) strategy in this article. The ASS strategy can identify the most promising subproblems in each iteration using the current population distribution and the historical information. 

In general, the SASOA builds an independent surrogate model for each of the selected subproblems [25]–[27]. The Gaussian process (GP) model (also known as Kriging [28]) is one of the most popular machine-learning models, as it can provide not only predictions but also their relevant uncertainty. Nevertheless, recent research reveals that the multioutput GP (MOGP) model makes better use of the correlation between the subproblems, thus can provide more accurate predictions [23], [29], [30]. As the GP model predicts the subproblem value at a new solution as a Gaussian distribution, we still need an acquisition function (also known as infill criterion) [24] to measure the new solution’s merit in optimizing the subproblem. Commonly used acquisition functions include the probability of improvement (PI) [32], the expected improvement (EI) [33], the lower confidence bound (LCB) [34], and so on. However, our investigation indicates that these successful single-objective acquisition functions are not perfect for multiobjective optimization. They may unduly encourage exploration when the training dataset contains many crowded nondominated objective vectors. We systematically integrate the ASS strategy, the CoMOGP model, and the ALCB into a novel algorithm called MOEA/D-ASS. ## _D. CoMOGP_ 

Given _T_ subproblems _g_ 1 _, . . . , gT_ , we can separately build an independent GP model for each of them. Or we can take each subproblem as one output and use an MOGP model to jointly model them [29]. The MOGP model can capture the subproblem correlations and transfer useful information across outputs. If there are similarities between the subproblems, the MOGP model is expected to provide more accurate predictions in comparison to modeling the subproblems individually. In [23], one MOGP model, that is, the multitask GP model [53], has been demonstrated to be effective in modeling similar adjacent subproblems. Nevertheless, when the similarity between the subproblems is weak, the prediction quality of the multitask GP model deteriorates severely. This is because the multitask GP model has only one shared process and cannot capture the individual characteristics of each output. 

Since the similarity between these selected subproblems cannot be accurately known, the choice of the surrogate model becomes important. In this study, we employ the CoMOGP [31] model to simultaneously model the subproblems. The CoMOGP model uses _Q_ shared processes to help transfer knowledge across subproblems and employs _T_ output-specific processes to catch the individual feature of each subproblem. Therefore, the CoMOGP model can provide high-quality predictions regardless of whether the similarity between the subproblems is strong or weak.

## III. ADAPTIVE SUBPROBLEM SELECTION 

## _A. Motivation_ 

- In each iteration, the SASOA selects one or multiple sub- 

- problems to conduct surrogate-assisted optimization. That is: 

- 1) uses the training data (i.e., the evaluated solutions) to build the subproblems’ surrogate models; 

- 2) locates some promising solutions with the prediction of the surrogate model; 

- 3) reevaluates the located solutions with the original expensive subproblems; 

- 4) updates the training data with these newly evaluated solutions. 

- From the perspective of subproblem selection, the existing 

SASOAs can be roughly classified into two categories. 

- 1) Some SASOAs conduct surrogate-assisted optimization on only a small number of subproblems in each iteration. These subproblems are selected in a random or sequential manner [23], [27]. As a consequence, the selected subproblems may be unnecessary<sup>2</sup> (e.g., _g_ 4 in Fig. 2) or resolved<sup>3</sup> (e.g., _g_ 1, _g_ 2, and _g_ 7 in Fig. 2). Operating on such subproblems usually results in undesirable solutions (e.g., solutions are very close to the evaluated ones). The re-evaluation of these undesirable solutions can cause severe inefficiency in the context of expensive optimization. 

- 2) The other SASOAs model the whole set of subproblems for optimization [21], [22], [54], and thus obtain a large set of candidate solutions. After that, these algorithms screen out a few most promising solutions from these candidate solutions. Obviously, the surrogate-assisted optimization performed by these SASOAs on many subproblems is wasteful. Moreover, this kind of posterior screening strategy may cause

Step 1: _Subproblem Prescreening_ 

- Step 1.1: Set _GI_ to _G_ and compute the distance matrix _D_ between _EP_ and _GI_ [cf., (20)]. 

- Step 1.2: Associate each objective vector to its closest subproblem according to _D_ , and remove the unassociated subproblems from _GI_ .

- Step 2: _Solving Degree Calculation_ 

- Step 2.1: Identify the nondominated set NP from _EP_ and obtain the distance relationship between NP and _GI_ . 

- Step 2.2: For each **f** ∈ NP, sort the subproblems of _GI_ in descending order according to their distances to **f** . 

- Step 2.3: Compute the solving degree (SD) value for each subproblem in _GI_ [cf., (21)]. 

- Step 3: _Subproblem Selection_

## VI. DISCUSSION 

In this section, we conduct a quantitative experimental analysis on the effectiveness of the ASS strategy, the CoMOGP model, and the ALCB acquisition function. To this end, we develop three variants of MOEA/D-ASS, that is: 1) Variant-RSS; 2) Variant-InGP; and 3) Variant-LCB. Variant-RSS differs from MOEA/D-ASS only in the selection of subproblems. Variant-RSS randomly selects subproblems in each iteration. The difference between Variant-InGP and MOEA/D-ASS lies in the choice of the surrogate model. In Variant-InGP, the subproblems are modeled individually using the GP model. Variant-LCB keeps the same as MOEA/D-ASS except that its acquisition function is changed to the LCB. As suggested in [65], the parameter _γ_ in Variant-LCB is set to 2. The three variants, along with MOEA/D-ASS, are tested on the ZDT problems. Their experimental results in terms of the IGD metric are provided in Table IX of the Supplemental File. 

## _A. ASS Versus Random Subproblem Selection_ 

The advantages of the ASS strategy can be demonstrated by comparing the results obtained by MOEA/D-ASS and Variant-RSS. From the results in Table IX of the Supplemental File, we can see that MOEA/D-ASS significantly outperforms Variant-RSS on ZDT1–3 and ZDT6. The advantages of MOEA/D-ASS are more evident on the problems with irregular PFs (i.e., ZDT3 and ZDT6). To further illustrate the effectiveness of the ASS strategy, we compare the two algorithms on the other three test problems with irregular PF shapes, that is, GLT1–GLT3 [66]. The number of decision variables _d_ is set to 8, and each algorithm is independently performed 21 times on each problem. The obtained IGD metric values are given in Table X of the Supplemental File. It clearly shows that MOEA/D-ASS is remarkably better than Variant-RSS on the three problems. To conclude, the ASS strategy does improve the SASOA’s performance, especially in addressing MOPs with complex PF shapes. 

## _B. CoMOGP Versus Independent GPs_ 

From the results in Table IX of the Supplemental File, we can also observe that the performance of MOEA/D-ASS is better than Variant-InGP on all the five problems. Considering that the two algorithms differ only in the choice of the surrogate model, we may safely draw the conclusion that

The strengths of the ALCB acquisition function can be illustrated by comparing the performance of MOEA/D-ASS and Variant-LCB, as they are only different in the acquisition function. Nevertheless, the current ALCB acquisition function is not yet a perfect one. The perfect ALCB acquisition function should take full advantage of the population distribution information in both the decision space and the objective space. More advanced ALCB acquisition functions will be studied in our future work. 

## _D. Impacts of the Setting of η_ 

In (21), _η_ determines how much we believe a subproblem is solved. If _η_ takes a very large value, only the subproblem with the closest nondominated objective vector is considered to be solved, that is, _gi_ ∈ _GI_ is considered to be solved only when there exists at least one _ri,j_ = | _GI_ | for **f**<sup>_j_</sup> ∈ NP. If _η_ adopts a small value, the subproblems that have the second or third closest nondominated objective vector (i.e., _ri,j_ is less than but close to | _GI_ |) may also be treated as solved ones. A larger _η_ can promote the algorithm to perform a fine-grained search, but if _η_ is too large, it may cause the solving degree of the subproblems to be indistinguishable. To investigate the impacts of _η_ on the performance of MOEA/D-ASS, we have considered four values for _η_ : 20, 50, 80, and 100. MOEA/D-ASS with these four different _η_ settings is examined on ZDT1 and ZDT2. The obtained mean IGD values over 21 independent runs are shown in Fig. 9. It indicates that _η_ = 100 is the best for MOEA/D-ASS, which confirms that our parameter setting is reasonable.

## VII. CONCLUSION 

In this article, we have pointed out that the existing SASOAs may work on some unnecessary subproblems or solved subproblems, thus resulting in computational inefficiency. To overcome this shortcoming, we have developed the ASS strategy. In each iteration, the ASS strategy can identify and select the most promising subproblems for further surrogate-assisted optimization. To better model the selected subproblems, the CoMOGP model has been introduced in the proposed algorithm MOEA/D-ASS. The CoMOGP model has both the output-specific processes and the shared processes, enabling it not only to capture the specific feature of each subproblem but also to transfer useful information across subproblems. Regardless of whether the similarity between the subproblems is strong or weak, the CoMOGP model can always maintain a good prediction quality. Furthermore, we have investigated the acquisition function and found that the commonly used acquisition functions are not perfect for multiobjective optimization. These acquisition functions may unduly encourage exploration when the training dataset contains many nondominated objective vectors. To better balance exploration and exploitation in multiobjective optimization scenarios, we have proposed a new acquisition function, namely, the ALCB. 

In the experimental study, we have compared MOEA/D-ASS with six representative algorithms on three different sets of test problems. The results have shown that MOEA/D-ASS is very competitive. Moreover, we have quantitatively validated the effectiveness of the ASS strategy, the CoMOGP model, and the ALCB acquisition function one by one. 

In the future, we will study the performance of MOEA/D-ASS on problems with higher dimensional variables/objectives.

I. INTRODUCTION
M
ANY real-world optimization problems involve more
than one objective [1]–[4]. Such problems are gen-
erally formulated as multiobjective optimization problems
(MOPs). Moreover, if the objective function evaluation of
candidate solutions is computationally or ﬁnancially costly
(e.g., it requires time-consuming simulations or expensive
physical experiments), the problems are referred to as expen-
sive MOPs [5]–[7]. Many surrogate-assisted optimization
algorithms have been developed to cope with expensive
MOPs [8]–[19]. Among them, some algorithms scalarize
the MOP into a number of single-objective subproblems
for optimization [20]–[24]. We refer to these algorithms
as surrogate-assisted scalarization optimization algorithms
(SASOAs), which are the focus of this article.
The SASOA builds surrogate models (also known as meta-
models) for the subproblems and uses the surrogate models
instead of the original subproblems to evaluate solutions.
Since the evaluation cost of the surrogate models is much
cheaper than that of the subproblems, the SASOA can sig-
niﬁcantly reduce the computational and/or ﬁnancial burden.
In each iteration, the SASOA selects one or multiple sub-
problems to conduct surrogate-assisted optimization. However,
these selected subproblems may be unnecessary or resolved
(cf., Section III-A). Operating on such subproblems has low
efﬁciency, especially in the scenario of expensive optimization.
To overcome this drawback, we propose an adaptive subprob-
lem selection (ASS) strategy in this article. The ASS strategy
can identify the most promising subproblems in each iteration
using the current population distribution and the historical
information.

uncertainty. Nevertheless, recent research reveals that the mul-
tioutput GP (MOGP) model makes better use of the correlation
between the subproblems, thus can provide more accurate
predictions [23], [29], [30]. This article creatively uses a col-

D. CoMOGP
Given T subproblems g1, . . . , gT, we can separately build an
independent GP model for each of them. Or we can take each
subproblem as one output and use an MOGP model to jointly
model them [29]. The MOGP model can capture the subprob-
lem correlations and transfer useful information across outputs.
If there are similarities between the subproblems, the MOGP
model is expected to provide more accurate predictions in
comparison to modeling the subproblems individually. In [23],
one MOGP model, that is, the multitask GP model [53], has
been demonstrated to be effective in modeling similar adjacent
subproblems. Nevertheless, when the similarity between the
subproblems is weak, the prediction quality of the multitask
GP model deteriorates severely. This is because the multitask
GP model has only one shared process and cannot capture the
individual characteristics of each output.
Since the similarity between these selected subproblems
cannot be accurately known, the choice of the surrogate
model becomes important. In this study, we employ the
CoMOGP [31] model to simultaneously model the subprob-
lems. The CoMOGP model uses Q shared processes to
help transfer knowledge across subproblems and employs T
output-speciﬁc processes to catch the individual feature of
each subproblem. Therefore, the CoMOGP model can provide
high-quality predictions regardless of whether the similarity
between the subproblems is strong or weak.

III. ADAPTIVE SUBPROBLEM SELECTION
A. Motivation
In each iteration, the SASOA selects one or multiple sub-
problems to conduct surrogate-assisted optimization. That is:
1) uses the training data (i.e., the evaluated solutions) to
build the subproblems’ surrogate models;
2) locates some promising solutions with the prediction of
the surrogate model;
3) reevaluates the located solutions with the original expen-
sive subproblems;
4) updates the training data with these newly evaluated
solutions.
From the perspective of subproblem selection, the existing
SASOAs can be roughly classiﬁed into two categories.
1) Some SASOAs conduct surrogate-assisted optimization
on only a small number of subproblems in each iteration.
These subproblems are selected in a random or sequen-
tial manner [23], [27]. As a consequence, the selected
subproblems may be unnecessary2 (e.g., g4 in Fig. 2) or
resolved3 (e.g., g1, g2, and g7 in Fig. 2). Operating on
such subproblems usually results in undesirable solu-
tions (e.g., solutions are very close to the evaluated
ones). The re-evaluation of these undesirable solutions
can cause severe inefﬁciency in the context of expensive
optimization.
2) The other SASOAs model the whole set of sub-
problems for optimization [21], [22], [54], and thus
obtain a large set of candidate solutions. After that,
these algorithms screen out a few most promising
solutions from these candidate solutions. Obviously,
the surrogate-assisted optimization performed by these
SASOAs on many subproblems is wasteful. Moreover,
this kind of posterior screening strategy may cause

VI. DISCUSSION
In this section, we conduct a quantitative experimen-
tal analysis on the effectiveness of the ASS strategy, the
CoMOGP model, and the ALCB acquisition function. To
this end, we develop three variants of MOEA/D-ASS, that
is: 1) Variant-RSS; 2) Variant-InGP; and 3) Variant-LCB.
Variant-RSS differs from MOEA/D-ASS only in the selection
of subproblems. Variant-RSS randomly selects subproblems
in each iteration. The difference between Variant-InGP and
MOEA/D-ASS lies in the choice of the surrogate model. In
Variant-InGP, the subproblems are modeled individually using
the GP model. Variant-LCB keeps the same as MOEA/D-ASS
except that its acquisition function is changed to the LCB. As
suggested in [65], the parameter γ in Variant-LCB is set to 2.
The three variants, along with MOEA/D-ASS, are tested on
the ZDT problems. Their experimental results in terms of the
IGD metric are provided in Table IX of the Supplemental File.
A. ASS Versus Random Subproblem Selection
The advantages of the ASS strategy can be demonstrated
by comparing the results obtained by MOEA/D-ASS and
Variant-RSS. From the results in Table IX of the Supplemental
File, we can see that MOEA/D-ASS signiﬁcantly outper-
forms Variant-RSS on ZDT1–3 and ZDT6. The advantages
of MOEA/D-ASS are more evident on the problems with
irregular PFs (i.e., ZDT3 and ZDT6). To further illustrate
the effectiveness of the ASS strategy, we compare the two
algorithms on the other three test problems with irregular PF
shapes, that is, GLT1–GLT3 [66]. The number of decision
variables d is set to 8, and each algorithm is independently
performed 21 times on each problem. The obtained IGD met-
ric values are given in Table X of the Supplemental File. It
clearly shows that MOEA/D-ASS is remarkably better than
Variant-RSS on the three problems. To conclude, the ASS
strategy does improve the SASOA’s performance, especially
in addressing MOPs with complex PF shapes.
B. CoMOGP Versus Independent GPs
From the results in Table IX of the Supplemental File, we
can also observe that the performance of MOEA/D-ASS is
better than Variant-InGP on all the ﬁve problems. Considering
that the two algorithms differ only in the choice of the
surrogate model, we may safely draw the conclusion that

compared with the individually built GP models.
C. ALCB Versus LCB
The strengths of the ALCB acquisition function can be illus-
trated by comparing the performance of MOEA/D-ASS and
Variant-LCB, as they are only different in the acquisition func-
tion. The results in Table IX of the Supplemental File indicate
that MOEA/D-ASS performs better than Variant-LCB on all
the ﬁve problems. Thereby we may reach the conclusion that
the ALCB acquisition function is more suitable than the LCB
in dealing with MOPs.
The effectiveness of the ALCB acquisition function indi-
cates that it is beneﬁcial to consider the population distribution
in the objective space in balancing between exploration and
exploitation. Nevertheless, the current ALCB acquisition func-
tion is not yet a perfect one. The perfect ALCB acquisition
function should take full advantage of the population distribu-
tion information in both the decision space and the objective
space. More advanced ALCB acquisition functions will be
studied in our future work.
D. Impacts of the Setting of η
In (21), η determines how much we believe a subproblem is
solved. If η takes a very large value, only the subproblem with
the closest nondominated objective vector is considered to be
solved, that is, gi ∈GI is considered to be solved only when
there exists at least one ri,j = |GI| for fj ∈NP. If η adopts
a small value, the subproblems that have the second or third
closest nondominated objective vector (i.e., ri,j is less than but
close to |GI|) may also be treated as solved ones. A larger η
can promote the algorithm to perform a ﬁne-grained search,
but if η is too large, it may cause the solving degree of the
subproblems to be indistinguishable. To investigate the impacts
of η on the performance of MOEA/D-ASS, we have consid-
ered four values for η: 20, 50, 80, and 100. MOEA/D-ASS
with these four different η settings is examined on ZDT1 and
ZDT2. The obtained mean IGD values over 21 independent
runs are shown in Fig. 9. It indicates that η = 100 is the best
for MOEA/D-ASS, which conﬁrms that our parameter setting
is reasonable.

VII. CONCLUSION
In this article, we have pointed out that the existing SASOAs
may work on some unnecessary subproblems or solved sub-
problems, thus resulting in computational inefﬁciency. To
overcome this shortcoming, we have developed the ASS strat-
egy. In each iteration, the ASS strategy can identify and select
the most promising subproblems for further surrogate-assisted
optimization. To better model the selected subproblems, the
CoMOGP model has been introduced in the proposed algo-
rithm MOEA/D-ASS. The CoMOGP model has both the
output-speciﬁc processes and the shared processes, enabling
it not only to capture the speciﬁc feature of each subproblem
but also to transfer useful information across subproblems.
Regardless of whether the similarity between the subproblems
is strong or weak, the CoMOGP model can always maintain a
good prediction quality. Furthermore, we have investigated the
acquisition function and found that the commonly used acqui-
sition functions are not perfect for multiobjective optimization.
These acquisition functions may unduly encourage exploration
when the training dataset contains many nondominated objec-
tive vectors. To better balance exploration and exploitation in
multiobjective optimization scenarios, we have proposed a new
acquisition function, namely, the ALCB.

by one.
