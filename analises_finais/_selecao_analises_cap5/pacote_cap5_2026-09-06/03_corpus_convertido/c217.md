## **Abstract** 

Multi-objective optimization problems in many real-world applications are characterized by computationally or economically expensive objectives, which cannot provide sufficient function evaluations for evolutionary algorithms to converge. Thus, a variety of surrogate models have been employed to provide much more virtual evaluations. Most existing surrogate models are essentially regressors or classifiers, which may suffer from low reliability in the approximation of complex objectives. In this paper, we propose a novel surrogate-assisted evolutionary algorithm, which employs a surrogate model to conduct pairwise comparisons between candidate solutions, rather than directly predicting solutions’ fitness values. In comparison to regression and classification models, the proposed pairwise comparison based model can better balance between positive and negative samples, and may be directly used, reversely used, or ignored according to its reliability in model management. As demonstrated by the experimental results on abundant benchmark and real-world problems, the proposed surrogate model is more accurate than popular surrogate models, leading to performance superiority over state-of-the-art surrogate models. 

_Keywords:_ evolutionary algorithms, expensive multi-objective optimization, surrogate-assisted optimization, pairwise comparison 

## **1. Introduction** 

Many scientific and engineering optimization problems contain multiple objectives to be optimized simultaneously [16, 47], which are known as multi-objective optimization problems (MOPs). An unconstrained MOP can be mathematically defined as: 



where **x** denotes a solution containing _d_ decision variables and _F_ ( **x** ) denotes its objective vector containing _m_ objective values. Due to the conflicting nature between objectives, it is impossible to obtain a unique optimal solution that can minimize all objectives. By contrast, there exist multiple Pareto optimal solutions trading off between all objectives, where an improvement on one objective is always accompanied by the deterioration of another. 

Owing to the population based search paradigm, evolutionary algorithms have shown efficiency in solving MOPs, which can generate a set of diverse solutions in a single run [56]. After decades of development, multiobjective evolutionary algorithms (MOEAs) have suggested various strategies for offspring generation (e.g., genetic operators [9], differential evolution [21], and particle swarm optimization [12]) and environmental selec- 

tion (e.g., non-dominated sorting [53], objective decomposition [51], and indicator based selection [39]). Given a randomly initialized population, MOEAs use it to generate new solutions via offspring generation strategies, and retain better solutions via environmental selection strategies. After repeating the above two operations for many generations, the population can be driven toward the Pareto front with good convergence and diversity. 

Since most MOEAs search in the decision space without using problem-dependent information, new solutions are iteratively generated to minimize the objective values. Obviously, such a trial-and-error procedure consumes a large number of objective function evaluations, making MOEAs inefficient when compared with other optimizers (e.g., mathematical programming methods [38]). Miserably, many engineering optimization problems in the real world involve time- or cost-consuming numerical simulations, where sufficient function evaluations are unaffordable. For example, many MOPs such as drug design [11] and aerodynamic design optimization [19] can only provide hundreds to thousands of function evaluations, whereas MOEAs generally require tens of thousands to millions of function evaluations [9]. To cope with computationally or economically expensive MOPs, surrogate models have been built to provide virtual evaluations for MOEAs [5, 33]. By learning the mappings between decision vectors and objective values based on a few evaluated solutions at hand, surrogateassisted MOEAs can perform environmental selection with the assistance of virtual evaluations, thus overcoming the barrier of expensive function evaluations [20].

The utilization of surrogate models is not straightforward. First, suitable models should be carefully selected for a high learning performance. Currently, Kriging models (i.e., Gaussian processes) [37], polynomial regression [57], support vector machines [6], radial basis function networks [48], and artificial neural networks [52] are popular surrogate models with MOEAs. Second, the inputs of surrogate models are always the decision vectors of solutions, while the outputs should be carefully defined. In existing surrogateassisted MOEAs, the outputs are generally objective values of solutions [4, 14] or labels indicating whether solutions are good or bad [50, 30]. That is, surrogate models are essentially used as regressors or classifiers. Third, after evaluating all the candidate solutions via surrogate models, a few solutions should be selected to be re-evaluated on the real objectives, which is known as model management. Considering the quality and uncertainty of candidate solutions, the probability of improvement [25], the expected improvement [29], and the lower confidence bound [24] are popular metrics used in model management. 

While the objectives of many MOPs consist of complex functions and datasets, surrogate models may encounter difficulties in learning the mappings based on just a few training samples [20]. To address the limitations of regression and classification based surrogate models used in existing MOEAs, this work proposes a pairwise comparison based surrogate model. Compared with regression models, the proposed surrogate model can be ignored or reversely used if the degree of reliability is unpromising, while regression models are always used even if they are unreliable. In comparison to classification models, the proposed surrogate model can better balance between positive and negative samples, which leads to robust performance when handling complex MOPs. Recently, several pairwise comparison based surrogate models have been suggested for singleor multi-objective optimization [28, 15]. However, these models do not consider the reliability of models, and are unable to achieve significantly better performance than regression and classification models as presented in the experiments [15]. 

To summarize, the main contributions of this work include the following two aspects: 

1. A pairwise comparison based surrogate model is proposed for multi-objective optimization, which is based on a probabilistic neural network whose input is the decision vectors of two solutions and output is a label indicating the better one. To train the surrogate model, all the evaluated solutions are assessed by a delicate fitness function considering both convergence and diversity, and good solutions and bad solutions are paired off as training samples. While existing work rarely compared the learning performance of surrogate models, this work performs experiments to verify that the proposed surrogate model has higher accuracies than existing models. 

2. A surrogate-assisted MOEA is proposed by inte- 

- grating the proposed surrogate model with a novel model management strategy, termed pairwise comparison based surrogate-assisted evolutionary algorithm (PC-SAEA). After training a surrogate model at the beginning of each generation, the proposed MOEA measures the degree of reliability based on validation samples. Then, if the degree of reliability is very high, the model is directly used to select candidate solutions. If the degree of reliability is very low, the model is reversely used to select candidate solutions. If the degree of reliability is insignificant, the model is not used since it currently makes predictions at random. In the experiments, the proposed MOEA exhibits better performance than state-of-the-art surrogate-assisted MOEAs on a variety of benchmark and real-world MOPs. 

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces existing surrogate models and analyzes their shortcomings, then raises the motivation of this work. Section 3 describes the details of the proposed surrogate model and algorithm. Section 4 conducts comparative experiments on benchmark and real-world problems. Finally, conclusions and future work are given in Section 5. 

## **2. Related Work and Motivation** 

## _2.1. Surrogate Models Used in Existing MOEAs_ 

A surrogate model aims to approximate a function _h_ ( **x** ) calculated based on the objective values _f_ 1( **x** ), . . . , _fm_ ( **x** ). According to the functions to be approximated, the surrogate models used in existing MOEAs can be roughly grouped into three categories, i.e., regression models, classification models, and pairwise comparison based models. 

Regression models receive the most attention from MOEAs, in which the approximated function is an objective function (i.e., _h_ 1( **x** ) = _f_ 1( **x** ), . . . , _hm_ ( **x** ) = _fm_ ( **x** )) or a fitness function derived from all the objective functions (i.e., _h_ ( **x** ) = _fitness_ ( _f_ 1( **x** ), . . . , _fm_ ( **x** ))). The idea of regression based surrogate models is straightforward as illustrated in Fig. 1, where the output consists of the objective values or fitness values that can be directly used in environmental selection. In K-RVEA [4], one Kriging model is built for approximating each objective function and multiple Kriging models work together for handling many-objective optimization problems. In EDNARMOEA [14], a dropout neural network is designed to tackle many objectives and decision variables, where the output of the surrogate model contains all the objective values. In TIC-SMEA [22], a convergence based criterion and a diversity based criterion for evaluating solutions are designed and estimated by Kriging models. To pursue robustness, HeEMOEA [13] builds multiple surrogate models for ensemble. 

Classification models are also widely employed by MOEAs, in which the approximated function is a mapping between a decision vector and a label, i.e., _h_ ( **x** ) = _class_ ( _f_ 1( **x** ), . . . , _fm_ ( **x** )) and its function value is binary or

## _2.2. Motivation_ 

All the above surrogate models have shown effectiveness in handling expensive MOPs, however, regression models and classification models generally have limitations on complex functions. For regression models, although they can estimate the uncertainty via probabilistic surrogates like Kriging models, it is difficult to quantify how accurate the surrogate is. Although regression

models can be assessed by some metrics such as RMSE, it is difficult to know whether a specific RMSE value indicates that the model is accurate or not. By contrast, it is easy to quantify how accurate a binary classifier is by checking whether its accuracy is significantly higher than 0.5. In this case, classification models and pairwise comparison based models will be ignored if their accuracies are not higher than 0.5; however, regression models always have to be used since their accuracies are unknown. In addition, classification models and pairwise comparison based models can be reversely used (i.e., reversing the predicted label) if their accuracies are extremely low [30], while regression models cannot. 

On the other hand, classification models also encounter difficulties in practice. First, it is difficult to suggest a suitable fitness function for dividing all the solutions into multiple classes, which should strike a balance between the numbers of positive and negative samples. An imbalanced dataset could highly deteriorate the performance of classifiers. On the contrary, pairwise comparison based models are naturally fed with the same numbers of positive and negative samples, since each pair of solutions are used as two samples ⟨ **x** , **y** ⟩, ⟨ **y** , **x** ⟩ with opposite labels. Second, assuming that _N_ solutions are evaluated on the real objectives for training a surrogate model, there will be _N_ training samples for a classification model and _N_ ( _N_ − 1) training samples for a pairwise comparison based model. Usually, more training samples lead to better performance. Third, classification models predict whether a candidate solution is similar to the better solutions (i.e., positive label) or the worse solutions (i.e., negative label) evaluated so far, which means that they cannot identify candidate solutions that are better than all the evaluated solutions. In contrast to classification models, pairwise comparison based models can identify whether a candidate solution **o** is better than the best evaluated solution **x** by feeding the sample ⟨ **o** , **x** ⟩. 

Although pairwise comparison based models have been recently employed by a few MOEAs, they just exhibit similar performance to regression and classification models [15], since the rough fitness functions and model management strategies obscure the advantages of pairwise comparison based models in expensive multiobjective optimization. More specifically, existing fitness functions are not tailored for pairwise comparison based models, which may not be able to well balance between the convergence and diversity of solutions. Besides, existing model management strategies do not consider the reliability of surrogate models, where surrogate models are always used and turn out to be uncertain. To fully exploit the potential of pairwise comparison based models, this work uses a fitness function considering both the convergence and diversity of solutions, so that the population consisting of the candidate solutions selected by the surrogate model can converge toward the Pareto front with good diversity. ## **3. The Proposed Algorithm** 

## _3.1. Procedure of PC-SAEA_ 

The procedure of the proposed surrogate-assisted evolutionary algorithm PC-SAEA is illustrated in Fig. 4 and Algorithm 1. To begin with, a population consisting of 11 _d_ −1 ( _d_ is the number of decision variables) solutions is generated using the Latin hypercube sampling [26] (Line 1), which is a conventional setting adopted in many existing works [4, 13, 30, 14, 15]. These initial solutions are evaluated on the real objectives (Line 2) and copied to an external archive (Line 4), which stores all the real-evaluated solutions. At each generation, some solutions are firstly selected from the current population for the training, validation, and prediction of a surrogate model (Line 6), and the training samples and validation samples are constructed accordingly (Line 7). Then, a pairwise comparison based surrogate model is trained (Line 8) and validated (Line 9) based on the constructed samples. Afterwards, a large number of offspring solutions are generated by genetic operators and filtered out by the surrogate model (Line 10), where a few of them is further evaluated on the real objectives (Line 11) and saved to the archive (Line 12). Lastly, the population for the next generation is obtained by selecting at most _N_ solutions from the archive via the environmental selection strategy of SPEA2 [58] (Line 13), and the number of real-evaluated solutions _FE_ is increased by | _Q_ | (Line 14).

## _3.3. Reliability Measurement of Surrogate Model_ 

While a surrogate model may not always be welltrained, it is reasonable to measure its reliability before using it. Basically, the surrogate model is not used if the degree of its reliability is unpromising. For this aim, the classification accuracy on validation set is calculated as the degree of reliability. As described in Algorithm 4, for each validation sample ⟨ **x** , **y** , 1⟩, it is correctly classified by the surrogate model if the predicted label of ⟨ **x** , **y** ⟩ is 1 and the predicted label of ⟨ **y** , **x** ⟩ is 0, and it is reversely classified if the predicted label of ⟨ **x** , **y** ⟩ is 0 and the predicted label of ⟨ **y** , **x** ⟩ is 1. While if both the predicted labels of ⟨ **x** , **y** ⟩ and ⟨ **y** , **x** ⟩ are 0 or 1, the surrogate model makes a contradiction and is likely to be useless.

## _3.4. Offspring Generation and Filtration_ 

After the training and validation of the surrogate model, it can be used to filter out a large number of newly generated candidate solutions, so that most candidate solutions are eliminated without expensive function evaluations. As described in Algorithm 5, the candidate population _Q_ is initialized to the same as the current population _P_ and evolved for _Gmax_ generations. When the surrogate model should be directly used (i.e., _state_ = 1), _Q_ is first combined with _Pbest_ and used to generate | _Pbest_ | offspring solutions via simulated binary crossover [7] and polynomial mutation [8]. Then, _Q_ is combined with the offspring solutions, and | _Pbest_ | solutions with the largest _score_ are selected from the combined population for the next generation. The _score_ of each candidate solution indicates its quality estimated by the surrogate model, which is calculated as described in Algorithm 6. For each candidate solution **x** , a solution **y** is randomly selected from _Pbest_ , and _score_ is set to the difference between the outputs of samples ⟨ **x** , **y** ⟩ and ⟨ **y** , **x** ⟩ calculated by (3). Obviously, a larger _score_ indicates a higher probability that the candidate solution **x** is better than the best evaluated solution **y** . By contrast, if a classification based surrogate model is adopted, it can only detect whether **x** has a similar quality to **y** , but cannot detect whether **x** is better than **y** , hence the population may not be able to converge quickly.

## **4. Empirical Studies** 

In this section, a series of experiments are performed to verify the superiority of the proposed surrogate model and algorithm. To be specific, the proposed PC-SAEA is first compared with six representative surrogate-assisted MOEAs on benchmark and realworld expensive MOPs. Then, the accuracy of the proposed surrogate model is compared with those used in existing MOEAs. Lastly, the effectiveness of the core components of PC-SAEA is verified by ablation studies. All the experiments are conducted on PlatEMO [40, 46].

## **5. Conclusions** 

To address the limitations of regression models and classification models in expensive multi-objective optimization, this paper has proposed a pairwise comparison based surrogate model, which compares the quality of two solutions in terms of a fitness function considering both convergence and diversity. To tackle the uncertainty in surrogate training, this paper has suggested a novel model management strategy, which directly uses the surrogate model, reversely uses the surrogate model, or ignores the surrogate model according to its degree of reliability. The experiments have verified that the proposed model is more accurate than state-of-the-art models for expensive multi-objective optimization, and the proposed algorithm outperforms state-of-the-art algorithms on benchmark and real-world problems. 

This paper has focused on the fitness function and model management of surrogate-assisted MOEAs, while the selection of surrogate models deserves to be further studied. On the one hand, the surrogate model used in this work is a simple probabilistic neural network, and more delicate machine learning models such as RankNet [2] and those used in existing algorithms [42] can be adopted. On the other hand, specific surrogate models can be customized to solve the expensive MOPs with special characteristics, such as dynamic MOPs [54] and multimodal MOPs [41].

tens of thousands to millions of function evaluations [9].

mappings between decision vectors and objective values

The utilization of surrogate models is not straight-
forward.
First, suitable models should be carefully
selected for a high learning performance.
Currently,
Kriging models (i.e., Gaussian processes) [37], polyno-
mial regression [57], support vector machines [6], ra-
dial basis function networks [48], and artiﬁcial neu-
ral networks [52] are popular surrogate models with
MOEAs.
Second, the inputs of surrogate models are
always the decision vectors of solutions, while the out-
puts should be carefully deﬁned. In existing surrogate-
assisted MOEAs, the outputs are generally objective val-
ues of solutions [4, 14] or labels indicating whether solu-
tions are good or bad [50, 30]. That is, surrogate models
are essentially used as regressors or classiﬁers. Third,
after evaluating all the candidate solutions via surro-
gate models, a few solutions should be selected to be
re-evaluated on the real objectives, which is known as
model management. Considering the quality and un-
certainty of candidate solutions, the probability of im-
provement [25], the expected improvement [29], and the
lower conﬁdence bound [24] are popular metrics used in
model management.
While the objectives of many MOPs consist of com-
plex functions and datasets, surrogate models may en-
counter difﬁculties in learning the mappings based on
just a few training samples [20]. To address the limi-
tations of regression and classiﬁcation based surrogate
models used in existing MOEAs, this work proposes a
pairwise comparison based surrogate model. Compared
with regression models, the proposed surrogate model
can be ignored or reversely used if the degree of reli-
ability is unpromising, while regression models are al-
ways used even if they are unreliable. In comparison
to classiﬁcation models, the proposed surrogate model
can better balance between positive and negative sam-
ples, which leads to robust performance when handling
complex MOPs. Recently, several pairwise comparison
based surrogate models have been suggested for single-
or multi-objective optimization [28, 15]. However, these
models do not consider the reliability of models, and are
unable to achieve signiﬁcantly better performance than
regression and classiﬁcation models as presented in the
experiments [15].
To summarize, the main contributions of this work in-
clude the following two aspects:
1. A pairwise comparison based surrogate model is
proposed for multi-objective optimization, which is
based on a probabilistic neural network whose in-
put is the decision vectors of two solutions and out-
put is a label indicating the better one.
To train
the surrogate model, all the evaluated solutions are
assessed by a delicate ﬁtness function considering
both convergence and diversity, and good solutions
and bad solutions are paired off as training samples.
While existing work rarely compared the learning
performance of surrogate models, this work per-
forms experiments to verify that the proposed sur-
rogate model has higher accuracies than existing
models.
2. A surrogate-assisted MOEA is proposed by inte-
grating the proposed surrogate model with a novel
model management strategy, termed pairwise com-
parison based surrogate-assisted evolutionary algo-
rithm (PC-SAEA). After training a surrogate model
at the beginning of each generation, the proposed
MOEA measures the degree of reliability based on
validation samples. Then, if the degree of reliabil-
ity is very high, the model is directly used to select
candidate solutions. If the degree of reliability is
very low, the model is reversely used to select can-
didate solutions. If the degree of reliability is in-
signiﬁcant, the model is not used since it currently
makes predictions at random. In the experiments,
the proposed MOEA exhibits better performance
than state-of-the-art surrogate-assisted MOEAs on
a variety of benchmark and real-world MOPs.

introduces existing surrogate models and analyzes their
shortcomings, then raises the motivation of this work.
Section 3 describes the details of the proposed surro-
gate model and algorithm. Section 4 conducts compar-
ative experiments on benchmark and real-world prob-
lems. Finally, conclusions and future work are given in
Section 5.
2. Related Work and Motivation
2.1. Surrogate Models Used in Existing MOEAs
A surrogate model aims to approximate a func-
tion h(x) calculated based on the objective values
f1(x), . . . , fm(x). According to the functions to be approx-
imated, the surrogate models used in existing MOEAs
can be roughly grouped into three categories, i.e., regres-
sion models, classiﬁcation models, and pairwise com-
parison based models.
Regression models receive the most attention from
MOEAs, in which the approximated function is an ob-
jective function (i.e., h1(x) = f1(x), . . . , hm(x) = fm(x)) or a
ﬁtness function derived from all the objective functions
(i.e., h(x) = fitness(f1(x), . . . , fm(x))). The idea of regres-
sion based surrogate models is straightforward as illus-
trated in Fig. 1, where the output consists of the objec-
tive values or ﬁtness values that can be directly used
in environmental selection. In K-RVEA [4], one Krig-
ing model is built for approximating each objective func-
tion and multiple Kriging models work together for han-
dling many-objective optimization problems. In EDN-
ARMOEA [14], a dropout neural network is designed to
tackle many objectives and decision variables, where the
output of the surrogate model contains all the objective
values. In TIC-SMEA [22], a convergence based criterion
and a diversity based criterion for evaluating solutions
are designed and estimated by Kriging models. To pur-
sue robustness, HeEMOEA [13] builds multiple surro-
gate models for ensemble.
Classiﬁcation models are also widely employed by
MOEAs, in which the approximated function is a map-
ping between a decision vector and a label, i.e., h(x) =

2.2. Motivation
All the above surrogate models have shown effective-
ness in handling expensive MOPs, however, regression
models and classiﬁcation models generally have limita-
tions on complex functions. For regression models, al-
though they can estimate the uncertainty via probabilis-
tic surrogates like Kriging models, it is difﬁcult to quan-
tify how accurate the surrogate is. Although regression

it is difﬁcult to know whether a speciﬁc RMSE value in-

it is easy to quantify how accurate a binary classiﬁer is
by checking whether its accuracy is signiﬁcantly higher

In this case, classiﬁcation models and pair-
wise comparison based models will be ignored if their
accuracies are not higher than 0.5; however, regression
models always have to be used since their accuracies are
unknown. In addition, classiﬁcation models and pair-
wise comparison based models can be reversely used
(i.e., reversing the predicted label) if their accuracies are
extremely low [30], while regression models cannot.
On the other hand, classiﬁcation models also en-
counter difﬁculties in practice. First, it is difﬁcult to sug-
gest a suitable ﬁtness function for dividing all the solu-
tions into multiple classes, which should strike a balance
between the numbers of positive and negative samples.
An imbalanced dataset could highly deteriorate the per-
formance of classiﬁers. On the contrary, pairwise com-
parison based models are naturally fed with the same
numbers of positive and negative samples, since each
pair of solutions are used as two samples ⟨x, y⟩, ⟨y, x⟩
with opposite labels. Second, assuming that N solutions
are evaluated on the real objectives for training a surro-
gate model, there will be N training samples for a classi-
ﬁcation model and N(N −1) training samples for a pair-
wise comparison based model. Usually, more training
samples lead to better performance. Third, classiﬁcation
models predict whether a candidate solution is similar
to the better solutions (i.e., positive label) or the worse
solutions (i.e., negative label) evaluated so far, which
means that they cannot identify candidate solutions that
are better than all the evaluated solutions. In contrast to
classiﬁcation models, pairwise comparison based mod-
els can identify whether a candidate solution o is better
than the best evaluated solution x by feeding the sam-
ple ⟨o, x⟩.
Although pairwise comparison based models have
been recently employed by a few MOEAs, they just ex-
hibit similar performance to regression and classiﬁca-
tion models [15], since the rough ﬁtness functions and
model management strategies obscure the advantages of
pairwise comparison based models in expensive multi-
objective optimization. More speciﬁcally, existing ﬁtness
functions are not tailored for pairwise comparison based
models, which may not be able to well balance between
the convergence and diversity of solutions. Besides, ex-
isting model management strategies do not consider the
reliability of surrogate models, where surrogate models
are always used and turn out to be uncertain. To fully
exploit the potential of pairwise comparison based mod-
els, this work uses a ﬁtness function considering both
the convergence and diversity of solutions, so that the
population consisting of the candidate solutions selected
by the surrogate model can converge toward the Pareto
front with good diversity. Moreover, this work suggests
a reliability based model management strategy, where
the surrogate model is directly used, reversely used, or
ignored according to its degree of reliability calculated

3. The Proposed Algorithm
3.1. Procedure of PC-SAEA
The procedure of the proposed surrogate-assisted
evolutionary algorithm PC-SAEA is illustrated in Fig. 4
and Algorithm 1. To begin with, a population consisting
of 11d−1 (d is the number of decision variables) solutions
is generated using the Latin hypercube sampling [26]
(Line 1), which is a conventional setting adopted in
many existing works [4, 13, 30, 14, 15]. These initial so-
lutions are evaluated on the real objectives (Line 2) and
copied to an external archive (Line 4), which stores all
the real-evaluated solutions. At each generation, some
solutions are ﬁrstly selected from the current population
for the training, validation, and prediction of a surrogate
model (Line 6), and the training samples and validation
samples are constructed accordingly (Line 7). Then, a
pairwise comparison based surrogate model is trained
(Line 8) and validated (Line 9) based on the constructed
samples. Afterwards, a large number of offspring solu-
tions are generated by genetic operators and ﬁltered out
by the surrogate model (Line 10), where a few of them
is further evaluated on the real objectives (Line 11) and
saved to the archive (Line 12). Lastly, the population for
the next generation is obtained by selecting at most N
solutions from the archive via the environmental selec-
tion strategy of SPEA2 [58] (Line 13), and the number of
real-evaluated solutions FE is increased by |Q| (Line 14).

neural network (PNN) [35] is adopted as the surrogate
model, which is ﬂexible to handle various encodings

3.4. Offspring Generation and Filtration
After the training and validation of the surrogate
model, it can be used to ﬁlter out a large number of
newly generated candidate solutions, so that most can-
didate solutions are eliminated without expensive func-
tion evaluations. As described in Algorithm 5, the can-
didate population Q is initialized to the same as the
current population P and evolved for Gmax generations.
When the surrogate model should be directly used (i.e.,
state = 1), Q is ﬁrst combined with Pbest and used to
generate |Pbest| offspring solutions via simulated binary
crossover [7] and polynomial mutation [8]. Then, Q is
combined with the offspring solutions, and |Pbest| solu-
tions with the largest score are selected from the com-
bined population for the next generation. The score of
each candidate solution indicates its quality estimated
by the surrogate model, which is calculated as described
in Algorithm 6. For each candidate solution x, a solu-
tion y is randomly selected from Pbest, and score is set to
the difference between the outputs of samples ⟨x, y⟩and
⟨y, x⟩calculated by (3). Obviously, a larger score indi-
cates a higher probability that the candidate solution x
is better than the best evaluated solution y. By contrast,
if a classiﬁcation based surrogate model is adopted, it
can only detect whether x has a similar quality to y, but
cannot detect whether x is better than y, hence the pop-
ulation may not be able to converge quickly.

5. Conclusions
To address the limitations of regression models and
classiﬁcation models in expensive multi-objective opti-
mization, this paper has proposed a pairwise compari-
son based surrogate model, which compares the quality
of two solutions in terms of a ﬁtness function consider-
ing both convergence and diversity. To tackle the uncer-
tainty in surrogate training, this paper has suggested a
novel model management strategy, which directly uses
the surrogate model, reversely uses the surrogate model,
or ignores the surrogate model according to its degree
of reliability.
The experiments have veriﬁed that the
proposed model is more accurate than state-of-the-art
models for expensive multi-objective optimization, and
the proposed algorithm outperforms state-of-the-art al-
gorithms on benchmark and real-world problems.
This paper has focused on the ﬁtness function
and model management of surrogate-assisted MOEAs,
while the selection of surrogate models deserves to be
further studied. On the one hand, the surrogate model
used in this work is a simple probabilistic neural net-
work, and more delicate machine learning models such
as RankNet [2] and those used in existing algorithms
[42] can be adopted. On the other hand, speciﬁc sur-
rogate models can be customized to solve the expen-
sive MOPs with special characteristics, such as dynamic
MOPs [54] and multimodal MOPs [41].
