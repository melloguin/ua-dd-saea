# Performance Indicator-Based Adaptive Model Selection for Offline Data-Driven Multiobjective Evolutionary Optimization 

Zhening Liu, Handing Wang , _Member, IEEE_ , and Yaochu Jin , _Fellow, IEEE_ 

**_Abstract_ —A number of real-world multiobjective optimization problems (MOPs) are driven by the data from experiments or computational simulations. In some cases, no new data can be sampled during the optimization process and only a certain amount of data can be sampled before optimization starts. Such problems are known as offline data-driven MOPs. Although multiple surrogate models approximating each objective function are able to replace the real fitness evaluations in evolutionary algorithms (EAs), their approximation errors are easily accumulated and therefore, mislead the solution ranking. To mitigate this issue, a new surrogate-assisted indicator-based EA for solving offline data-driven multiobjective problems is proposed. The proposed algorithm adopts an indicator-based selection EA as the baseline optimizer due to its selection robustness to the approximation errors of surrogate models. Both the Kriging models and radial basis function networks (RBFNs) are employed as surrogate models. An adaptive model selection mechanism is designed to choose the right type of models according to a maximum acceptable approximation error that is less likely to mislead the indicator-based search. The main idea is that when the uncertainty of the Kriging models exceeds the acceptable error, the proposed algorithm selects RBFNs as the surrogate models. The results comparing with state-of-the-art algorithms on benchmark problems with up to ten objectives indicate that the proposed algorithm is effective on offline data-driven optimization problems with up to 20 and 30 decision variables.** 

**_Index Terms_ —Indicator-based evolutionary algorithm (EA), Kriging models, model selection, offline data-driven multiobjective optimization, radial basis function networks (RBFNs), surrogate models.**

Because of the outstanding ability of multiobjective evolutionary algorithms (MOEAs) in handling black-box problems and their characteristics of generating multiple optimal solutions in one run, MOEAs gradually become a popular tool for MOPs [4]. Existing MOEAs can be generally divided into three classes: 1) Pareto dominance-; 2) aggregation-; and 3) performance indicator-based algorithms according to [5]. In addition, hybrid MOEAs have received great attention in recent years [6]. For example, the algorithms in MOEA/D-DE+PSO [7], AMALGAM [8], and ESEA [9] adopt different adaptive methods to combine and select baseline MOEAs, such as differential evolution [10], particle swam optimization [11], and genetic algorithms [12]. Another example called MOEA/D-DRA-CMX + SPX [13] adaptively selects different evolutionary operators in various situations.

Most existing MOEAs consider that the real fitness values are cheap to calculate. However, most fitness evaluations can only be obtained from expensive experiments or computer simulations in some real-world applications. Evolutionary optimization processes based on data collected from experiments, numerical simulations, or other resources are called data-driven evolutionary optimization [14]. Datadriven optimization could be divided into two different types according to whether the new data can be evaluated by the real fitness evaluations during the optimization process. One class in which a certain amount of new data can be sampled using expensive fitness evaluations during the evolutionary optimization process is called online data-driven evolutionary optimization [15]. Under this circumstance, traditional MOEAs are hardly applicable because of their dependence on the large number of real fitness evaluations [16]. Evolutionary algorithms (EAs) that use surrogate models (metamodels) approximating fitness evaluations to reduce the number of samples are known as surrogate-assisted EAs (SAEAs) [17]. Many successful SAEAs for expensive MOPs have been proposed [16], such as CSEA [18], K-RVEA [19], MOEA/DEGO [20], HSMEA [21], Par-EGO [22], and SAMSO [23]. 

However, in some situations, no additional proactive experiments or simulations (i.e., the real fitness evaluations) are allowed to perform during the optimization process, which is called offline data-driven evolutionary optimization. The difficulty is that only a small amount of historical offline data can be provided for offline data-driven evolutionary optimization, which cannot be solved by traditional MOEAs. For example, blast furnaces optimization contains more than 100 decision variables, such as quartzite, manganese, alkali, and alumina additives. The amount of these components decides the final product and economic requirements. However, performing blast furnaces systems is time consuming, complex, and costly. It is unrealistic to actively obtain the designated observation points by experiments during the optimization process [24]. Offline data-driven evolutionary optimization can be selected to solve this optimization problem. Another real-world problem is the optimization of fused magnesium furnaces, which involves the complex physical and chemical processes involved, intermittent material supplies, and sensor failures hinder the possibility of online sampling [25]. Thus, only historical production data can be utilized. To solve these challenging optimization problems, surrogates can be built based on the available data to perform offline data-driven evolutionary optimization. 

Such problems pose more challenges to SAEAs than the online data-driven optimization problems. Although existing SAEAs for online data-driven optimization can be applied to offline data-driven optimization, the performance of those SAEAs will degenerate because they heavily depend on the sampling strategy of online data rather than the model quality directly built from the offline data. Some SAEAs have been proposed for offline single-objective optimization. For example, DDEA-SE generates a large number of surrogate models using the offline data divided by the bagging technique [26] and adaptively selects a subset of models to estimate the fitness 

for the evolutionary optimization [27]. Another algorithm called SRK-DDEA uses four radial basis function networks (RBFNs) with different kernels as its surrogate models and then adopts the stochastic ranking method [28] to combine those four RBFNs [29] as the approximated fitness evaluations. 

Due to the confliction between multiple objectives, the approximation errors of the surrogate models will be magnified when employing the single-objective offline data-driven EAs for MOPs. Therefore, offline data-driven MOEAs require high-quality surrogate models. One effective approach is making full use of the offline data and generating multifidelity surrogates to balance the model quality and time complexity. That is the reason why SA-NSGA-II [30] takes the advantages of the multiple fidelity levels generated from the big offline data for the Scotland trauma system design problem. Unfortunately, big data are hard to be obtained for many offline data-driven MOPs. Therefore, how to enhance the accuracy and robustness of the surrogate models built from small offline data is the key issue of offline data-driven MOEAs. Recently, an algorithm proposed in [31] directly uses the Kriging models based on the reference vector-guided EA (RVEA) [32], and it smooths the noisy offline data to solve the complex blast furnace optimization problem. However, this algorithm still does not consider the approximation errors of the Kriging models. NSGA-II-GP employs the Gaussian process to assist NSGA-II [33] with the expected improvement (EI) [34] as its online data sampling criterion [25]. Then, loworder polynomial regression models built on the offline data will evaluate these online data in order to enrich the offline data. This presents an interesting idea, since it enables us to use many online data-driven MOEAs for solving offline problems. However, the required data amount for building loworder polynomial regression models makes NSGA-II-GP hard to solve high-dimensional problems and the inaccurate surrogates may mislead the optimization direction. The algorithms in [35] combine all the uncertainty information of the Kriging models with their objectives for stable solutions and store them in one archive to select the final results. Even so, the inaccurate predicted objectives in the archive could have negative impact on the performance. 

Although numerous of surrogate models have been employed in SAEAs, such as Kriging [36], RBFNs [37], artificial neural networks [38], and polynomial regression [39], there does not exist an algorithm that can select different models for different offline data-driven optimization problems. Among these surrogate models, the Kriging and RBFN models are popular because they adopt a spatial interpolation based on the hypothesis that close locations have similar labels [40]. This hypothesis enables them to achieve reliable approximation to the fitness function. In addition, the structures of the Kriging and RBFN models are similar, while the difference in their methods for determining the hyperparameters results in different performance and complexity. The RBFN model is computationally more efficient than the Kriging model; however, the Kriging model can provide the uncertainty information (a confidence interval) about its predictions while the RBFN model cannot.

Offline data-driven optimization problems are commonly seen in the real world and are very challenging. In view of the above challenges, a new indicator-based offline datadriven MOEA with an adaptive model selection strategy (called IBEA-MS) is proposed to solve offline data-driven optimization problems for the following two main reasons. 

- 1) The indicator-based selection EA (IBEA _ϵ_ + ) is chosen as the baseline search method because it has been proved to be an effective performance indicator-based MOEA suited both for multiobjective and offline data-driven optimization. The experimental results obtained in this work have confirmed the effectiveness of IBEA _ϵ_ + . 

- 2) A plethora of existing work on data-driven optimization has shown that the Kriging and RBFN models are two most effective models as surrogates, but they have their respective advantages for different problems. Out of the above reasons, an adaptive model selection mechanism is designed that selects either the Kriging or RBFN models based on an acceptable reliability tolerance during the process of IBEA _ϵ_ + combining the _Iϵ_ + calculation. This way, the proposed algorithm is able to make the best use of the Kriging and RBFN models on different optimization problems.

The reason of selecting IBEA _ϵ_ + and a discussion about the pros and cons of the Kriging and RBFN models when they are used as surrogate models are also briefly explained. The details of our proposed algorithm are described in Section III. Numerical experiments and the analysis of the results are detailed in Section IV. Finally, Section V concludes this article and outlines future research work.

Therefore, there is a tradeoff between the complexity and accuracy in hyperparameter optimization for the Kriging model and RBFN.

## IV. NUMERICAL EXPERIMENTS 

The code of experiments is developed based on the PlatEMO platform [51]. The performance of our proposed algorithm will be tested in this section. NSGA-II-GP [25] and three algorithms in [35] (called AK-IBEA variants) are chosen as the algorithms for comparison. These algorithms can be summarized as follows. 

- 1) _NSGA-II-GP:_ This algorithm is based on NSGA-II with a two-layer evolutionary process called the outer and inner layers. In the outer layer, a second-order polynomial regression model is built on the offline data to generate synthetic data for enriching the training data for the Kriging model in the inner layer. In the inner layer, NSGA-II-GP performs NSGA-II assisted by the Kriging models to select _Nk_ individuals with maximal EI values. The outer layer reevaluates those _Nk_ individuals and adds them into the training data. At the very end of the algorithm, the environmental selection operation in NSGA-II is applied to the training data for selecting the optimal individuals. 

- 2) _AK-IBEA Variants:_ The Kriging models are adopted to approximate each objective in AK-IBEA variants to assist IBEA _ϵ_ + . In that work, three methods with different use of the uncertainty information in the Kriging models are discussed. AK-IBEA1 uses the predicted RMSEs of all the Kriging models as additional objectives. Thus, the number of objectives is expanded to 2 _M_ , where _M_ is the number of the problem objectives. Similar to AKIBEA1, the mean predicted RMSEs of all the Kriging models is added as an extra objective in AK-IBEA2, which means the number of objectives is expanded to _M_ + 1. AK-IBEA3 employs the EI criteria of all the Kriging models as _M_ objectives. In addition, all the three AK-IBEA variants add the evaluated individuals to an archive and use the environmental selection operation in NSGA-II for the archives to select their final outputs. 

Various benchmark suites are performed for validation experiments in this article, which are DTLZ1-7 [52], ZDT1-4, ZDT6 [53], IDTLZ1-2 [54], WFG1-9 [55], UF1-12 [56], and MaF1-9 [57] problems. The detailed experimental settings are shown as follows. 

- 1) _Initial Offline Data:_ The number of individuals provided by users as the offline data is set as 11 _D_ − 1, where _D_ is the number of decision variables. The sampling techniques for the offline data are Latin hypercube sampling (LHS) [58]. In addition, the robustness of the proposed algorithm to the initial offline data is also examined and analyzed, which is presented in the supplementary material. 

- 2) _Reproduction Operations:_ In this work, SBX and the polynomial mutation are employed to generate the offspring, where their distribution factors are set to 20. The crossover probability is 1 and the mutation probability is 1 _/D_ . 

- 3) _Population Size:_ The populations in our proposed algorithm and AK-IBEA variants have 100 individuals. It means they will provide 100 individuals as the final results. Since NSGA-II-GP adopts a two-layer evolutionary process and saves all resampled individuals for the outer layer, the population size in the original paper is maintained and 100 final results are selected for comparison. In the inner layer of NSGA-II-GP, the population size of NSGA-II is set to 50 and the _Nk_ individuals selected for the outer loop is set to 5 as [25].

Overall, the good performance of IBEA-MS can be attributed to the following three aspects. 

- 1) The choice of the indicator-based selection EA (IBEA _ϵ_ + ) reduces the influence of the approximation errors of the surrogate models. 

- 2) The Kriging models and RBFNs are built to provide their respective advantages for different problems. 

- 3) An adaptive model selection mechanism is proposed to select more appropriate models between the Kriging models and RBFNs on different problems. 

There are also some limitations of IBEA-MS. On the one hand, only the Kriging and RBFN models are chosen to approximate the objective functions in this article. However, both of these surrogate models may be inaccurate because of the limited initial offline data provided by the users. On the other hand, the predicted values and predicted variances of the Kriging models are unreliable when the dimension of the decision space is high. This may mislead the proposed adaptive model selection mechanism. 

How to design offline data-driven MOEAs is concluded in the following. The designing process needs to carefully consider the following aspects: model validation, surrogate management, and the selection of the final output. First, the accuracy of the surrogate models should be verified during the optimization process to guarantee a right search direction. In fact, the variance of the Kriging model can provide such information without sampling new data. Second, although it is impossible to update surrogate models by new samples, surrogate management strategies are important for offline data-driven MOEAs. Finally, without any chance for verifying the optimal solutions obtained during the search, the choice of the final output is a challenging task. Most offline data-driven EAs directly select the achieved optimal solutions or use the solutions generated in the last generation as the final output. However, these solutions are not reliable in the offline datadriven optimization cases. The algorithm in [66] employs a new strategy that groups all the achieved solutions and averages each group as the final output, which reduces the errors introduced by surrogate models. 

Finally, how offline data-driven MOEAs should be applied to real-world problems is briefly discussed below. Offline datadriven optimization problems are a kind of special real-world applications, where only an offline dataset (a number of real function evaluations) is provided before the optimization process for finding an optimum or optimal solutions. For example, the blast furnaces optimization problem [31] as a classical offline data-driven MOP needs extremely time-consuming and costly experiment to complete the evaluation of one decision vector. Thus, a set of initial offline data is accumulated from long-term productions and experiments, which is the input of the optimizers to obtain their predicted optimum. According to the results of Table S.VIII in the supplementary material, it has little effect on the algorithm’s performance whether the offline data are collected by adopting LHS or random sampling. Then, offline data-driven MOEAs can be performed based on the constructed surrogate models and output a set of optimal solutions for the users. Taking the blast furnaces optimization problem as an example, the output solutions are consisted by a number of solutions with their predicted objective values. Finally, users choose a subset of or all output solutions for final decision making according to their own needs. It is believed that different benchmarks can, more or less, reflect different aspects of challenges in real-world applications. Therefore, the algorithm’s performance on the benchmark problems can show the effectiveness of the algorithm on the real applications to a certain degree. 

## V. CONCLUSION 

In this work, a new indicator-based surrogate-assisted EA using a model selection mechanism is proposed for solving offline data-driven MOPs. IBEA-MS builds the Kriging models and RBFNs based on the offline data for each objective, respectively, and introduces an adaptive model selection mechanism during the optimization process. The Kriging models are replaced by the RBFNs if they exceed the reliability tolerance of the adaptive model selection. IBEA-MS is compared with three AK-IBEA variants and NSGA-II-GP on widely used two-, three-, five-, and ten-objective DTLZ and ZDT problems with 10, 20, and 30 decision variables. The results indicate that IBEA-MS has the best overall performance when the numbers of decision variables are 10, 20, and 30.

I. INTRODUCTION
T
HE OBJECTIVES of many optimization problems are
usually conﬂicted with each other, which means per-
forming the optimization of one objective may worsen some
other objectives. Such problems are called multiobjective
optimization problems (MOPs) [1]. MOPs are very common
in the real world, especially in engineering applications [2].
These practical problems are usually very complicated, which
makes the multiobjective optimization an important research
ﬁeld [3]. The mathematical deﬁnition of an MOP can be
written as follows:
minimize F(xi) = (f1(xi), f2(xi), . . . , fM(xi))
subject to xi ∈S
(1)
where M is the number of objectives and xi is the decision
vector. S is the feasible space in the decision space. There are
multiple optimal solutions because the objectives are conﬂict-
ing. The set of solutions that cannot dominated by any other
feasible solutions is called the Pareto set (PS) and the set of
their corresponding objective values is called the Pareto front
(PF). A solution x1 dominating another solution x2 represents
that F(x1) is closer to the PF than F(x2), that is, x1 ≺x2. The
Pareto dominance relation can be deﬁned as follows:
x1 ≺x2 ⇔∀i ∈{1, 2, . . . , M}, fi(x1) ≤fi(x2) ∧
∃j ∈{1, 2, . . . , M}, fj(x1) < fj(x2).
(2)
If only ∀i ∈{1, 2, . . . , M}, fi(x1) ≤fi(x2) holds, x1 is called
weakly dominating x2, that is, x1 ⪯x2.
Because of the outstanding ability of multiobjective evolu-
tionary algorithms (MOEAs) in handling black-box problems
and their characteristics of generating multiple optimal solu-
tions in one run, MOEAs gradually become a popular tool
for MOPs [4]. Existing MOEAs can be generally divided
into three classes: 1) Pareto dominance-; 2) aggregation-;
and 3) performance indicator-based algorithms according
to [5]. In addition, hybrid MOEAs have received great atten-
tion in recent years [6]. For example, the algorithms in
MOEA/D-DE+PSO [7], AMALGAM [8], and ESEA [9]
adopt different adaptive methods to combine and select base-
line MOEAs, such as differential evolution [10], particle
swam optimization [11], and genetic algorithms [12]. Another
example called MOEA/D-DRA-CMX + SPX [13] adaptively
selects different evolutionary operators in various situations.

ues are cheap to calculate. However, most ﬁtness evalu-
ations can only be obtained from expensive experiments
or computer simulations in some real-world applications.
Evolutionary optimization processes based on data collected
from experiments, numerical simulations, or other resources
are called data-driven evolutionary optimization [14]. Data-
driven optimization could be divided into two different types
according to whether the new data can be evaluated by the
real ﬁtness evaluations during the optimization process. One
class in which a certain amount of new data can be sampled
using expensive ﬁtness evaluations during the evolutionary
optimization process is called online data-driven evolution-
ary optimization [15]. Under this circumstance, traditional
MOEAs are hardly applicable because of their dependence on
the large number of real ﬁtness evaluations [16]. Evolutionary
algorithms (EAs) that use surrogate models (metamodels)
approximating ﬁtness evaluations to reduce the number of
samples are known as surrogate-assisted EAs (SAEAs) [17].
Many successful SAEAs for expensive MOPs have been
proposed [16], such as CSEA [18], K-RVEA [19], MOEA/D-
EGO [20], HSMEA [21], Par-EGO [22], and SAMSO [23].
However, in some situations, no additional proactive exper-
iments or simulations (i.e., the real ﬁtness evaluations) are
allowed to perform during the optimization process, which is
called ofﬂine data-driven evolutionary optimization. The difﬁ-
culty is that only a small amount of historical ofﬂine data can
be provided for ofﬂine data-driven evolutionary optimization,
which cannot be solved by traditional MOEAs. For example,
blast furnaces optimization contains more than 100 deci-
sion variables, such as quartzite, manganese, alkali, and
alumina additives. The amount of these components decides
the ﬁnal product and economic requirements. However, per-
forming blast furnaces systems is time consuming, complex,
and costly. It is unrealistic to actively obtain the designated
observation points by experiments during the optimization
process [24]. Ofﬂine data-driven evolutionary optimization
can be selected to solve this optimization problem. Another
real-world problem is the optimization of fused magnesium
furnaces, which involves the complex physical and chemical
processes involved, intermittent material supplies, and sen-
sor failures hinder the possibility of online sampling [25].
Thus, only historical production data can be utilized. To solve
these challenging optimization problems, surrogates can be
built based on the available data to perform ofﬂine data-driven
evolutionary optimization.
Such problems pose more challenges to SAEAs than the
online data-driven optimization problems. Although existing
SAEAs for online data-driven optimization can be applied
to ofﬂine data-driven optimization, the performance of those
SAEAs will degenerate because they heavily depend on the
sampling strategy of online data rather than the model quality
directly built from the ofﬂine data. Some SAEAs have been
proposed for ofﬂine single-objective optimization. For exam-
ple, DDEA-SE generates a large number of surrogate models
using the ofﬂine data divided by the bagging technique [26]
and adaptively selects a subset of models to estimate the ﬁtness
for the evolutionary optimization [27]. Another algorithm
called SRK-DDEA uses four radial basis function networks
(RBFNs) with different kernels as its surrogate models and
then adopts the stochastic ranking method [28] to combine
those four RBFNs [29] as the approximated ﬁtness evaluations.
Due to the conﬂiction between multiple objectives, the
approximation errors of the surrogate models will be mag-
niﬁed when employing the single-objective ofﬂine data-driven
EAs for MOPs. Therefore, ofﬂine data-driven MOEAs require
high-quality surrogate models. One effective approach is mak-
ing full use of the ofﬂine data and generating multiﬁdelity
surrogates to balance the model quality and time complexity.
That is the reason why SA-NSGA-II [30] takes the advan-
tages of the multiple ﬁdelity levels generated from the big
ofﬂine data for the Scotland trauma system design problem.
Unfortunately, big data are hard to be obtained for many
ofﬂine data-driven MOPs. Therefore, how to enhance the accu-
racy and robustness of the surrogate models built from small
ofﬂine data is the key issue of ofﬂine data-driven MOEAs.
Recently, an algorithm proposed in [31] directly uses the
Kriging models based on the reference vector-guided EA
(RVEA) [32], and it smooths the noisy ofﬂine data to solve
the complex blast furnace optimization problem. However, this
algorithm still does not consider the approximation errors of
the Kriging models. NSGA-II-GP employs the Gaussian pro-
cess to assist NSGA-II [33] with the expected improvement
(EI) [34] as its online data sampling criterion [25]. Then, low-
order polynomial regression models built on the ofﬂine data
will evaluate these online data in order to enrich the ofﬂine
data. This presents an interesting idea, since it enables us
to use many online data-driven MOEAs for solving ofﬂine
problems. However, the required data amount for building low-
order polynomial regression models makes NSGA-II-GP hard
to solve high-dimensional problems and the inaccurate surro-
gates may mislead the optimization direction. The algorithms
in [35] combine all the uncertainty information of the Kriging
models with their objectives for stable solutions and store them
in one archive to select the ﬁnal results. Even so, the inaccu-
rate predicted objectives in the archive could have negative
impact on the performance.

A. Adaptive Model Selection
As mentioned in Section II-A, one major challenge
for ofﬂine data-driven multiobjective optimization is that
the approximation errors of surrogate models mislead the
optimization direction, because the poor individuals might be
assigned high ﬁtness values and mistakenly selected for the
next generation. Therefore, IBEAϵ+ is adopted as the baseline
optimization method and a mechanism considering the MSE
values of the Kriging models is proposed to mitigate the above
problem.
As shown in Section II-A, the dominance preserving rules of
IBEAϵ+ of any x1, x2, and x3 can be written as follows [41]:
(i) x1 ≺x2 ⇒I(x1, x2) < I(x2, x1)
(ii) x1 ≺x2 ⇒I(x3, x1) ≥I(x3, x2)

IV. NUMERICAL EXPERIMENTS
The code of experiments is developed based on the
PlatEMO platform [51]. The performance of our proposed
algorithm will be tested in this section. NSGA-II-GP [25] and
three algorithms in [35] (called AK-IBEA variants) are cho-
sen as the algorithms for comparison. These algorithms can
be summarized as follows.
1) NSGA-II-GP: This algorithm is based on NSGA-II with
a two-layer evolutionary process called the outer and
inner layers. In the outer layer, a second-order poly-
nomial regression model is built on the ofﬂine data to
generate synthetic data for enriching the training data
for the Kriging model in the inner layer. In the inner
layer, NSGA-II-GP performs NSGA-II assisted by the
Kriging models to select Nk individuals with maximal
EI values. The outer layer reevaluates those Nk individu-
als and adds them into the training data. At the very end
of the algorithm, the environmental selection operation
in NSGA-II is applied to the training data for selecting
the optimal individuals.
2) AK-IBEA Variants: The Kriging models are adopted
to approximate each objective in AK-IBEA variants to
assist IBEAϵ+. In that work, three methods with different
use of the uncertainty information in the Kriging mod-
els are discussed. AK-IBEA1 uses the predicted RMSEs
of all the Kriging models as additional objectives. Thus,
the number of objectives is expanded to 2M, where M
is the number of the problem objectives. Similar to AK-
IBEA1, the mean predicted RMSEs of all the Kriging
models is added as an extra objective in AK-IBEA2,
which means the number of objectives is expanded to
M + 1. AK-IBEA3 employs the EI criteria of all the
Kriging models as M objectives. In addition, all the
three AK-IBEA variants add the evaluated individuals
to an archive and use the environmental selection oper-
ation in NSGA-II for the archives to select their ﬁnal
outputs.
Various benchmark suites are performed for validation exper-
iments in this article, which are DTLZ1-7 [52], ZDT1-4,
ZDT6 [53], IDTLZ1-2 [54], WFG1-9 [55], UF1-12 [56], and
MaF1-9 [57] problems. The detailed experimental settings are
shown as follows.
1) Initial Ofﬂine Data: The number of individuals provided
by users as the ofﬂine data is set as 11D −1, where D
is the number of decision variables. The sampling tech-
niques for the ofﬂine data are Latin hypercube sampling
(LHS) [58]. In addition, the robustness of the proposed
algorithm to the initial ofﬂine data is also examined
and analyzed, which is presented in the supplementary
material.
2) Reproduction Operations: In this work, SBX and the
polynomial mutation are employed to generate the off-
spring, where their distribution factors are set to 20. The
crossover probability is 1 and the mutation probability
is 1/D.
3) Population Size: The populations in our proposed algo-
rithm and AK-IBEA variants have 100 individuals. It
means they will provide 100 individuals as the ﬁnal
results. Since NSGA-II-GP adopts a two-layer evo-
lutionary process and saves all resampled individuals
for the outer layer, the population size in the original
paper is maintained and 100 ﬁnal results are selected
for comparison. In the inner layer of NSGA-II-GP,
the population size of NSGA-II is set to 50 and the
Nk individuals selected for the outer loop is set to 5
as [25].

variants, the maximum number of allowed ﬁtness eval-

of generations is 50 in the original algorithm. This is
insufﬁcient. Thus, the maximum number of evaluations
is set as 50000 so that NSGA-II-GP in the inner layer
can run for 50 generations.

all the problems according to [41].

problem based on both IGD and HV performance and the com-
plete results are uploaded in the Github2. The above results
further prove that IBEA-MS performs better than AK-IBEA
variants as a whole.
Overall, the good performance of IBEA-MS can be
attributed to the following three aspects.
1) The
choice
of
the

selection
EA
(IBEAϵ+) reduces the inﬂuence of the approximation
errors of the surrogate models.
2) The Kriging models and RBFNs are built to provide
their respective advantages for different problems.
3) An adaptive model selection mechanism is proposed
to select more appropriate models between the Kriging
models and RBFNs on different problems.
There are also some limitations of IBEA-MS. On the one hand,
only the Kriging and RBFN models are chosen to approximate
the objective functions in this article. However, both of these
surrogate models may be inaccurate because of the limited
initial ofﬂine data provided by the users. On the other hand, the
predicted values and predicted variances of the Kriging models
are unreliable when the dimension of the decision space is
high. This may mislead the proposed adaptive model selection
mechanism.
How to design ofﬂine data-driven MOEAs is concluded
in the following. The designing process needs to carefully
consider the following aspects: model validation, surrogate
management, and the selection of the ﬁnal output. First, the
accuracy of the surrogate models should be veriﬁed during
the optimization process to guarantee a right search direc-
tion. In fact, the variance of the Kriging model can provide
such information without sampling new data. Second, although
it is impossible to update surrogate models by new sam-
ples, surrogate management strategies are important for ofﬂine
data-driven MOEAs. Ensemble learning could be an effec-
tive approach because the weights of diverse surrogate models
can be assigned adaptively to improve the accuracy of the
ensemble model [27]. Generating the synthetic data in model
management strategies has used for the ofﬂine data-driven
optimization [65]. In this work, a relatively reliable surrogate
model is selected by an adaptive model selection mechanism,

gate models. Finally, without any chance for verifying the
optimal solutions obtained during the search, the choice of
the ﬁnal output is a challenging task. Most ofﬂine data-driven
EAs directly select the achieved optimal solutions or use the
solutions generated in the last generation as the ﬁnal output.
However, these solutions are not reliable in the ofﬂine data-

new strategy that groups all the achieved solutions and aver-
ages each group as the ﬁnal output, which reduces the errors
introduced by surrogate models.
Finally, how ofﬂine data-driven MOEAs should be applied
to real-world problems is brieﬂy discussed below. Ofﬂine data-
driven optimization problems are a kind of special real-world
applications, where only an ofﬂine dataset (a number of real
function evaluations) is provided before the optimization pro-
cess for ﬁnding an optimum or optimal solutions. For example,
the blast furnaces optimization problem [31] as a classical
ofﬂine data-driven MOP needs extremely time-consuming and
costly experiment to complete the evaluation of one decision
vector. Thus, a set of initial ofﬂine data is accumulated from
long-term productions and experiments, which is the input of
the optimizers to obtain their predicted optimum. According to
the results of Table S.VIII in the supplementary material, it has
little effect on the algorithm’s performance whether the ofﬂine
data are collected by adopting LHS or random sampling. Then,
ofﬂine data-driven MOEAs can be performed based on the
constructed surrogate models and output a set of optimal solu-
tions for the users. Taking the blast furnaces optimization
problem as an example, the output solutions are consisted by
a number of solutions with their predicted objective values.
Finally, users choose a subset of or all output solutions for ﬁnal
decision making according to their own needs. It is believed
that different benchmarks can, more or less, reﬂect different
aspects of challenges in real-world applications. Therefore, the
algorithm’s performance on the benchmark problems can show
the effectiveness of the algorithm on the real applications to
a certain degree.
V. CONCLUSION
In this work, a new indicator-based surrogate-assisted EA
using a model selection mechanism is proposed for solving
ofﬂine data-driven MOPs. IBEA-MS builds the Kriging mod-
els and RBFNs based on the ofﬂine data for each objective,
respectively, and introduces an adaptive model selection mech-
anism during the optimization process. The Kriging models
are replaced by the RBFNs if they exceed the reliability toler-
ance of the adaptive model selection. IBEA-MS is compared
with three AK-IBEA variants and NSGA-II-GP on widely used
two-, three-, ﬁve-, and ten-objective DTLZ and ZDT problems
with 10, 20, and 30 decision variables. The results indicate that
IBEA-MS has the best overall performance when the numbers
of decision variables are 10, 20, and 30.

selection for ofﬂine data-driven multiobjective evolution-
