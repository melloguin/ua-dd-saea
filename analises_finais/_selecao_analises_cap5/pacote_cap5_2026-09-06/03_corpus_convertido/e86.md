# An Expensive Many-Objective Optimization Algorithm Based on Efficient Expected Hypervolume Improvement 

Yong Pang, Yitang Wang, Shuai Zhang, Xiaonan Lai, Wei Sun, and Xueguan Song 

**_Abstract_ —The expected hypervolume improvement (EHVI) is one of the most popular infill criteria for multiobjective optimization problems. Although it has a significant advantage in exploring potential Pareto-optimal solutions, it has rarely been applied in many-objective problems due to its high computational cost. To address this issue, this paper proposes an expensive manyobjective optimization algorithm based on the framework of NSGA-III and assisted by the kriging surrogate models. In the proposed algorithm, the Monte Carlo sampling method for EHVI estimation is improved by importance sampling, in which only one sampling process is required during the entire optimization process using a uniform distribution in normalized objective space. Considering the predicted uncertainty from the kriging model, an uncertainty-assisted nondominated sorting approach is proposed to substitute for the conventional approach in NSGA-III. In the proposed method, the predicted uncertainty is incorporated into the objective space as one independent dimension for nondominated sorting, which can enable the exploration of potential points with desirable EHVI values. In addition, the proposed algorithm considers the diversity of the solutions by deemphasizing the pursuit of the best EHVI. The experimental results on benchmark problems demonstrate that the proposed EHVI calculation method can save computational costs compared with Monte Carlo sampling and indicate the superiority of the proposed algorithm over the others.** 

**_Index Terms_ —Expected hypervolume improvement, expensive many-objective optimization, importance sampling, kriging.** 

## I. INTRODUCTION 

ULTIOBJECTIVE optimization problems (MOPs) have received much attention in recent years due to M the requirement for handling typical practical engineering problems with conflicting objectives. Specifically, MOPs are problems with no more than three objectives, while problems with more than three objectives are called manyobjective optimization problems (MaOPs). A large number of multiobjective evolutionary algorithms (MOEAs) and manyobjective evolutionary algorithms (MaOEAs) have been proposed for these problems, such as the nondominated sorting genetic algorithm II (NSGA-II) [10] and the multiobjective evolutionary algorithm based on decomposition (MOEA/D) [55] for MOPs and the nondominated sorting genetic algorithm III (NSGA-III) [9] and the improved two-archive algorithm (Two_Arch2) [33] for MaOPs. 

Considering the expensive evaluations of partial real-world responses, such as computational fluid dynamics, it is impractical to apply these MOEAs and MaOEAs directly to these expensive problems [36]. To address this issue, the surrogate model technique is introduced for evolutionary algorithms to replace the expensive objectives, yielding the surrogate-assisted evolutionary algorithm (SAEA) [25]. The most popular surrogate models include artificial neural networks [32], [52], radial basis functions [26], and kriging models [16], [18]. Based on these surrogate models, a considerable number of effective multiobjective SAEAs have been proposed, for instance, CSEA [35] for artificial neural networks, KRVEA [7] and KTA2 [40] for kriging models, and HSMEA [17] and SAEMO [58] for hybrid surrogate models. 

In addition to the surrogate model, a conventional multiobjective SAEA generally has two other major components worthy of study to improve its performance. The first is the multiobjective optimizer, which is commonly based on a mature optimization algorithm framework. For instance, the optimizer in KRVEA [7] is based on a MaOEA named RVEA [6], and the optimizer in KTA2 [40] is based on Two_Arch2 [33]. Another major component is the infill method, which adds more valuable samples to the training dataset in the process of optimization to improve the accuracy of the surrogate model and improve the solution to approximate the true optimum. In this paper, infill methods are classified into two categories for review: infill methods that use the uncertainty from the surrogate model and those that do not. 

_Infill methods without uncertainty_ : Uncertainty is an important property of the prediction from the surrogate model, as it can represent the confidence level of the prediction and is frequently used to guide the improvement of the accuracy of surrogate models and the exploration of unknown areas in the decision space. Methods that do not consider uncertainty generally assume that the prediction of the response from the surrogate models at an unknown point is reliable to pursue the maximum improvement of the optimal solution or PF. Additionally, some studies consider the convergence of the solutions based on the distance of the candidate points from an ideal point [7], [35], [41], [53], where a candidate point with a smaller distance yields a greater improvement for the convergence of the solution. In MOPs and MaOPs, diversity is another key element considered in infill without uncertainty. MOEAs based on reference vectors [7], [17] or weight decomposition [37] generally emphasize the diversity of the added points by clustering the population into subpopulations, where an appropriate point is selected in each cluster. Other algorithms often use the distance between the candidate points and the existing expensive samples to maintain diversity [40], [51]. 

_Infill methods with uncertainty_ : It seems that uncertaintybased infill methods are preferred because surrogate models are not perfect substitutes for real objective functions. With uncertainty, both the design and objective space can be explored more fully to improve the performance of the algorithm. The uncertainty from the surrogate model in the multiobjective SAEA can also be classified into two categories. The first uses different predictions by different types of surrogate models, such as the hybrid surrogate model-based algorithm SAEMO [58] and the random forest-assisted algorithm RF-CMOCO [45]. The greater the difference in the predictions, the higher the uncertainty. However, the application of this uncertainty is not widespread due to the prerequisites of the hybrid surrogate model. Another source of uncertainty is the mean-square error (MSE) [39] of the kriging model. Some MOEAs use the MSE directly as one of the infill criteria in MOPs and MaOPs [40], [43], [59]. More ingeniously, some MOEAs apply the expected improvement (EI) criterion [31] for infill, such as ParEGO [27], MOEA/D-EGO [56], and EIM-EGO[28], [54]. 

Expected hypervolume improvement (EHVI) is an extension of EI for multiobjective SAEA, which was first proposed by Emmerich [12]. Most studies on the exact calculation of EHVI focus on 2-D [13], [50], [14] and 3-D [47],[46] problems. The reason is that the partition of an irregular hypervolume in a high-dimensional objective space is very difficult. In addition, Couckuyt [8] _et al._ proposed an exact calculation method for EHVI in higher dimensions, but it did not give the exact time complexity 𝑂𝑂(𝑛𝑛log𝑛𝑛) and indicated that it is still unable to escape the curse of dimensionality. Additionally, in higher-dimensional cases, Yang [46] _et al._ developed an algorithm for -objective problems with time complexity ). In this context, the computational cost of higher-𝑂𝑂(𝑛𝑛<sup>⌊𝑑𝑑/2⌋</sup> dimensional problems is still great, even intractable. For this 

reason, the exact EHVI infill method can only be implemented in the SAEA for MOPs and not MaOPs. For example, in [30] and [49], EHVI was implemented as the infill criterion of the proposed MOEAs. In addition, EHVI was applied to solve some engineering optimization problems with a small number of objectives [34], [48]. 

Concerning the challenge of the exact calculation of the EHVI, the approximation method is more acceptable in highdimensional objective problems. In [12], Emmerich first recommended applying Monte Carlo sampling (MCS) to the EHVI calculation. Luo [29] _et al._ applied MCS for EHVI calculation and tested the method with some benchmark problems. Svenson and Santner [42] proposed a maximin fitness improvement function as the measurement for improving the solution and calculated the expected maximin fitness improvement to select points for infilling by MCS in higher dimensions as well. Abdolshah [1] _et al._ extended the EHVI to a situation in which the priority of a specific objective could be expressed, which was also calculated by the sampling method. Nevertheless, the calculation of the EHVI approximated by MCS is not as efficient as expected, so it has also never been introduced in the many-objective SAEA for MaOPs. The fundamental reason is that the MCS is a pointwise method; that is, for each point requiring EHVI calculations, a completely new set of samples with a large size should be generated. 

To address the issue of time-consuming EHVI calculations in high-dimensional-objective problems, a new approximate EHVI calculation method is proposed in this paper as the infill criterion for expensive MaOPs, and its efficiency is significantly improved by the importance sampling (IS) approach. Based on the framework of NSGA-III [9], the proposed MaOEA is named NSGAIII-EHVI. To allow the optimizer to select more potentially valuable individuals in evolution, a new uncertainty-assisted nondominated sorting and selection method is proposed to replace the common approach in NSGA-III. The main contributions of this work can be summarized as follows: 

- 1) An efficient EHVI calculation method for MaOPs with the assistance of IS is proposed in this paper. To reduce the number of sampling times for the EHVI calculation, the objective space of the population is normalized to a unified scale before infill. Additionally, IS is applied to the normalized objective space using a uniform distribution to replace the normal distribution from each individual. Using uniform samples obtained by IS has two advantages. The first advantage is that the uniform samples can be used to estimate the improvement in the hypervolume of the samples over the PF. The other is that the uniform samples can be used to calculate the EHVI on a unified scale. The proposed method requires only one round of sampling for EHVI calculation, which is dramatically less than that of the conventional MCS, which needs to sample based on every individual in every population. 

- 2) The proposed algorithm considers the positive effect of the predicted uncertainty from the kriging model on expensive MaOPs, for which a new nondominated sorting method is 

2 

proposed and applied to the evolution of NSGA-III. First, the population is divided into two subpopulations that are nondominated and dominated by the current PF. In the first subpopulation, the individuals with smaller objective values and smaller MSEs from the kriging models are preferentially selected, while in the second subpopulation, individuals with smaller objective values and larger MSEs are prioritized. The proposed sorting method can explore potentially valuable individuals in the population, including some individuals that are poorly predicted by the surrogate model in the response but have high predicted uncertainty. 

- 3) The proposed algorithm also balances the requirements of the convergence and diversity properties of the solution. The points added by the expensive function are quite valuable for small-sample problems because only one point is selected per iteration in NSGAIII-EHVI. First, the population is sorted according to the calculated EHVI. Then, the individuals with the best EHVIs are separated as the candidate solutions in a random proportion. Finally, the individual with the best diversity among the candidate solutions is selected. This method may seem simple, but it can effectively take diversity into account with negligible computational cost.

## _C. Effect of the Introduced Importance Sampling_ 

To investigate the effect of the applied IS on the performance and efficiency of the algorithm, the proposed algorithm with the MCS method suggested in [12] is developed and is named NSGAIII-EHVI3 as the compared algorithm. In these comparison experiments, the DTLZ test suite with 5 and 10 objectives is used for comparison without loss of generality. The number of decision variables in all the problems is set to 10. The experimental results are shown in Table V with metrics IGD, HV, and running time. 

For the IGD metric, the results of NSGAIII-EHVI3 on DTLZ5 and DTLZ6 are better than those of NSGAIII-EHVI, but this is not the case in the other problems. For the HV metric, the results of NSGAIII-EHVI3 are better than those of NSGAIII-EHVI on the whole. This indicates that the introduced IS method does not improve the accuracy of the proposed algorithm and even slightly worsens it. This is because of the 

variation in the contraction of the sampling area. MCS based on a normal distribution is conducted in an infinite area, while uniform sampling based on the IS is limited to the normalized area. Regarding the points near the bounds of the uniform distribution, the error of the EHVI estimation becomes large relative to the MCS method. 

Table V also provides the results of the running time. These results show an order-of-magnitude difference between MCS and IS. In each problem, NSGAIII-EHVI takes less than two minutes to finish the optimization, but NSGAIII-EHVI3 takes approximately one hour. Furthermore, the results indicate that the running time of the algorithm based on the MCS method is greatly affected by the number of objectives, but NSGAIIIEHVI is not. The reason is that the efficiency of the MCS method is related to the number of objectives because sampling is conducted independently for each objective of each individual. However, improved by IS, sampling in NSGAIIIEHVI is required only once. In this context, the impact of the 

9 

increased number of objectives on the computational cost is negligible. This also implies the usability of the proposed

## _D. Comparison with Other Algorithms_ 

In this section, the proposed NSGAIII-EHVI algorithm is compared with other state-of-the-art MOEAs. The compared algorithms are divided into two classes. The first class is the MOEA assisted by an EGO strategy, including ParEGO [27], SMS-EGO [38], and EIM-EGO [54], where SMS-EGO is based on the hypervolume infill criterion and EIM-EGO is based on the EI matrix criterion. The second class contains state-of-theart MaOEAs, including CSEA [35], KTA2 [40], K-RVEA [7], and HSMEA [17]. All the compared algorithms are implemented in PlatEMO [44] except for HSMEA. The number of objectives is set to 3, 5, 8, and 10 for each problem. The number of decision variables for all the test functions is set to 10 except for WFG2 and WFG3, with 8 and 10 objectives, respectively, for which the number of decision variables is set to 11. The IGD results on the DTLZ test suite and WFG test suite are shown in Table VI and Table VII, respectively, where the best results are bolded in each row and summed in the last row of the table with the results of the Wilcoxon test. Due to page limitations, the HV results are shown and discussed in the supplementary material. 

From the results of the DTLZ test suite in Table VI, it can be seen that the proposed NSGAIII-EHVI algorithm obtains 21 best results among 40 test problems, followed by KTA2 with 15 best results, KRVEA with 2 best results, and SMSEGO and HSMEA with only 1 best result. ParEGO, EIMEGO, and CSEA have no best results in the 40 test problems. Combining the 

results of the Wilcoxon test with respect to all the problems, it is concluded that the performance of the proposed algorithm is significantly better than those of the compared algorithms except for KTA2 on the DTLZ test suite. 

For the DTLZ1 and DTLZ3 problems, KTA2 provides the best results among all the algorithms. This can be explained by the fact that in DTLZ1 and DTLZ3, a multimodel function is introduced, resulting in numerous local Pareto-optimal solutions to the problems and hindering the convergence of the solution. Because EHVI considers both the convergence and uncertainty of the prediction, NSGAIII-EHVI performs more poorly than KTA2, as KTA2 emphasizes convergence more. Compared with the other methods except for KTA2, NSGAIIIEHVI presents slightly better results on DTLZ1 and DTLZ3. 

DTLZ2 and DTLZ4 are problems with a relatively simple fitness landscape and a spherical PF. NSGAIII-EHVI presents superior performance to the other methods on these problems but only on DTLZ2 with 5 and 8 decision variables, where KTA2 is better in terms of mean value. This indicates that the proposed NSGAIII-EHVI algorithm has the ability to obtain solutions with a good distribution. 

The PFs in problems DTLZ5-7 are irregular; the PF is a degenerate curve in DTLZ5, a set of disconnected regions in DTLZ6, and a combination of a straight line and a hyperplane in DTLZ7. The results in Table VI indicate that NSGAIII-EHVI performs better than the other methods on DTLZ6 and DTLZ7 but is worse on DTLZ5. The reason is most likely that it is difficult for NSGAIII-EHVI to converge to the PF with a curved shape in DTLZ5, as only a few reference points in NSGA-III are distributed near the curve. However, for DTLZ6 and DTLZ7, although the PF is irregular, the reference points in NSGA-III can cover most of the region. In this condition, NSGAIII-EHVI can use its ability to discover potential optimal points. Overall, the results indicate the feasibility of NSGAIIIEHVI for problems with irregular PFs. 

IDTLZ1-IDTLZ3 are three problems with inverted PFs. In these experiments, the proposed NSGAIII-EHVI obtains 7 best results, followed by KTA2 with 5 best results. The other algorithms yield no best results. This indicates that the proposed algorithm has competitive performance in the problems with inverted PFs in the DTLZ test suite and is appropriate for addressing these problems. 

The WFG test suite includes problems that exhibit properties that are not evident in the DTLZ test suite [20], such as being nonseparable and deceptive. This suggests that the WFG test suite can provide more challenges for MOEAs. The results in Table VII clearly show that NSGAIII-EHVI is significantly better than all other compared MOEAs on the WFG test suite, which is indicated not only by the best results but also by the Wilcoxon test. NSGAIII-EHVI obtains 36 best results out of 48 problems, followed by KTA2, with only 10 best results, and HSMEA, with only 2 best results. The Wilcoxon test results show that NSGAIII-EHVI is significantly better than KTA2 on nearly half of the problems but worse on only 4 problems. Regarding the other compared algorithms, the priority of NSGAIII-EHVI is more dominant. 

WFG1 and WFG3 are the only two problems on which KTA2 provides competitive results relative to NSGAIII-EHVI.

For WFG1, a number of transformation functions are introduced, making it difficult for MOEAs to maintain the diversity of the solutions [33]. Although NSGAIII-EHVI considers the diversity of the solutions, the emphasis is weaker than that of KTA2, which makes diversity one of the infill criteria. Nevertheless, NSGAIII-EHVI obtains two better results than KTA2 on WFG1. WFG3 is another version of WFG2 (which has a disconnected PF) with a continuous PF. NSGAIII-EHVI performs better than KTA2 on WFG2 but worse on WFG3. These results are somewhat similar to the results on DTLZ6 (with a disconnected PF), which show that NSGAIII-EHVI performs better than the others. This finding suggests that, compared to KTA2, NSGAIII-EHVI is more applicable to a complex problem with a disconnected PF. 

_Discussion_ : From the results on both the DTLZ and WFG test suites, it can be concluded that the proposed NSGAIII-EHVI algorithm shows superiority to and more comprehensive applicability than other MOEAs. Relative to MOEAs with the EGO strategy, including ParEGO, SMSEGO, and EIMEGO, the performance of NSGAIII-EHVI is better, which can be explained by two factors. The first is the superiority of the optimizer. The optimizers applied in ParEGO, SMSEGO, and EIMEGO are not designed for MaOPs. Hence, the evolution of the population in these algorithms considers less diverse solutions, resulting in the obtained candidate solutions for infill having poor diversity. The second factor is the superiority of the infill criterion. The EI criterion used in ParEGO and EIMEGO is calculated for the SOP, which is somewhat crude for direct application to MaOPs. Although SMSEGO selects 

points based on the hypervolume, the use of the predicted uncertainty from the surrogate model is poorer than that of EHVI. In contrast, EHVI can obtain more comprehensive information on the improvement of the solution over the current PF, as it is calculated directly in a high-dimensional objective space. In addition, the three compared algorithms do not consider the diversity of the points for infill. In this context, the solution obtained cannot be distributed well on the true PF. 

Comparing the results for three state-of-the-art expensive MaOPs with those of KTA2, CSEA, KRVEA, and HSMEA, the proposed algorithm still performs better. Because these methods are all designed for MaOPs, the main reason for this involves the difference in the infill criterion. CSEA and KRVEA have the same infill strategy, which is based on reference vectors as in the method of HSMEA. The infill strategies of these three algorithms take no account of the predicted uncertainty. They give enough confidence to the predictions of the surrogate model to select desirable points with good convergence and good diversity. This can lead to the selected points deviating greatly from the prediction and failing to yield the expected ideal solution. Although KTA2 designs an uncertainty requirement to select the points with the largest uncertainty, it does not embed this uncertainty into the mechanism for convergence, resulting in only the improvement of the surrogate model accuracy and not the improvement of the infill performance. However, the EHVI can consider the effect of the uncertainty predicted from the kriging surrogate model on the selection of the new point.

Combined with the proposed uncertainty-assisted nondominated sorting method, the proposed algorithm has a strong ability to discover the points with the potential to improve the current PF. Due to this discovery capability, NSGAIII-EHVI is more appropriate for expensive MaOPs, especially problems with a small sample size, where the surrogate model often fails to provide accurate predictions. 

## VI. CONCLUSION 

To address the challenge of applying the EHVI to expensive MaOPs, this paper proposes the NSGAIII-EHVI algorithm based on the NSGA-III framework. Focusing on the infill criterion, the traditional calculation of the EHVI based on MCS is improved by the IS method, which achieves a significant increase in the computational efficacy of EHVI calculation with only one sampling. To use the EHVI to select candidate infill points with better quality, an uncertainty-assisted nondominated sorting method is proposed. In this method, the uncertainty is considered a dimension of the objective space for sorting, and the population is divided into a dominated set and a nondominated set, thus deploying different sorting methods. In addition, the infill criterion of the proposed algorithm considers the diversity of the solutions, thereby obtaining a more reasonable distribution of the solutions. 

The performance of NSGAIII-EHVI was tested on DTLZ and WFG test suites. The experimental results show that the application of the IS can reduce the computational cost significantly with little loss of accuracy and illustrate that the uncertainty-assisted nondominated sorting method proposed in NSGAIII-EHVI outperforms the traditional method. The experimental results also demonstrate the superiority of NSGAIII-EHVI in small-sample problems compared to other algorithms and show the great potential of the EHVI method for expensive MaOPs. This work fully utilizes the positive effect of the prediction uncertainty on the convergence of the solution but does not consider the uncertainty of the diversity. Therefore, future work will focus on the diversity of the solutions based on uncertainty.

Abstract—The expected hypervolume improvement (EHVI) is 
one of the most popular infill criteria for multiobjective 
optimization problems. Although it has a significant advantage in 
exploring potential Pareto-optimal solutions, it has rarely been 
applied in many-objective problems due to its high computational 
cost. To address this issue, this paper proposes an expensive many-
objective optimization algorithm based on the framework of 
NSGA-III and assisted by the kriging surrogate models. In the 
proposed algorithm, the Monte Carlo sampling method for EHVI 
estimation is improved by importance sampling, in which only one 
sampling process is required during the entire optimization 
process using a uniform distribution in normalized objective 
space. Considering the predicted uncertainty from the kriging 
model, an uncertainty-assisted nondominated sorting approach is 
proposed to substitute for the conventional approach in NSGA-III. 
In the proposed method, the predicted uncertainty is incorporated 
into the objective space as one independent dimension for 
nondominated sorting, which can enable the exploration of 
potential points with desirable EHVI values. In addition, the 
proposed algorithm considers the diversity of the solutions by de-
emphasizing the pursuit of the best EHVI. The experimental 
results on benchmark problems demonstrate that the proposed 
EHVI calculation method can save computational costs compared 
with Monte Carlo sampling and indicate the superiority of the 
proposed algorithm over the others. 
Index Terms—Expected hypervolume improvement, expensive 
many-objective optimization, importance sampling, kriging. 
I. INTRODUCTION 
ULTIOBJECTIVE optimization problems (MOPs) 
have received much attention in recent years due to 
the requirement for handling typical practical 
engineering problems with conflicting objectives. Specifically, 
MOPs are problems with no more than three objectives, while 
problems with more than three objectives are called many-
objective optimization problems (MaOPs). A large number of 
multiobjective evolutionary algorithms (MOEAs) and many-
objective evolutionary algorithms (MaOEAs) have been 
proposed for these problems, such as the nondominated sorting 
genetic algorithm II (NSGA-II) [10] and the multiobjective 
evolutionary algorithm based on decomposition (MOEA/D) 
[55] for MOPs and the nondominated sorting genetic algorithm 
III (NSGA-III) [9] and the improved two-archive algorithm 
(Two_Arch2) [33] for MaOPs.

Considering the expensive evaluations of partial real-world 
responses, such as computational fluid dynamics, it is 
impractical to apply these MOEAs and MaOEAs directly to 
these expensive problems [36]. To address this issue, the 
surrogate model technique is introduced for evolutionary 
algorithms to replace the expensive objectives, yielding the 
surrogate-assisted evolutionary algorithm (SAEA) [25]. The 
most popular surrogate models include artificial neural 
networks [32], [52], radial basis functions [26], and kriging 
models [16], [18]. Based on these surrogate models, a 
considerable number of effective multiobjective SAEAs have 
been proposed, for instance, CSEA [35] for artificial neural 
networks, KRVEA [7] and KTA2 [40] for kriging models, and 
HSMEA [17] and SAEMO [58] for hybrid surrogate models. 
In addition to the surrogate model, a conventional 
multiobjective SAEA generally has two other major 
components worthy of study to improve its performance. The 
first is the multiobjective optimizer, which is commonly based 
on a mature optimization algorithm framework. For instance, 
the optimizer in KRVEA [7] is based on a MaOEA named 
RVEA [6], and the optimizer in KTA2 [40] is based on 
Two_Arch2 [33]. Another major component is the infill 
method, which adds more valuable samples to the training 
dataset in the process of optimization to improve the accuracy 
of the surrogate model and improve the solution to approximate 
the true optimum. In this paper, infill methods are classified into 
two categories for review: infill methods that use the 
uncertainty from the surrogate model and those that do not. 
Infill methods without uncertainty: Uncertainty is an 
important property of the prediction from the surrogate model, 
as it can represent the confidence level of the prediction and is 
frequently used to guide the improvement of the accuracy of 
surrogate models and the exploration of unknown areas in the 
decision space. Methods that do not consider uncertainty 
generally assume that the prediction of the response from the 
surrogate models at an unknown point is reliable to pursue the 
maximum improvement of the optimal solution or PF. Without 
considering the uncertainty of the prediction, the most 
reasonable method of infill is to select desirable points with 
optimal properties, such as methods based on the dominated

Infill methods with uncertainty: It seems that uncertainty-
based infill methods are preferred because surrogate models are 
not perfect substitutes for real objective functions. With 
uncertainty, both the design and objective space can be 
explored more fully to improve the performance of the 
algorithm. The uncertainty from the surrogate model in the 
multiobjective SAEA can also be classified into two categories. 
The first uses different predictions by different types of 
surrogate models, such as the hybrid surrogate model-based 
algorithm SAEMO [58] and the random forest-assisted 
algorithm RF-CMOCO [45]. The greater the difference in the 
predictions, the higher the uncertainty. However, the 
application of this uncertainty is not widespread due to the 
prerequisites of the hybrid surrogate model. Another source of 
uncertainty is the mean-square error (MSE) [39] of the kriging 
model. Some MOEAs use the MSE directly as one of the infill 
criteria in MOPs and MaOPs [40], [43], [59]. More ingeniously, 
some MOEAs apply the expected improvement (EI) criterion 
[31] for infill, such as ParEGO [27], MOEA/D-EGO [56], and 
EIM-EGO[28], [54]. 
Expected hypervolume improvement (EHVI) is an extension 
of EI for multiobjective SAEA, which was first proposed by 
Emmerich [12]. Most studies on the exact calculation of EHVI 
focus on 2-D [13], [50], [14] and 3-D [47],[46] problems. The 
reason is that the partition of an irregular hypervolume in a 
high-dimensional objective space is very difficult. The exact 
calculation of EHVI was first reported in [13] with a time 
complexity of 𝑂𝑂(𝑛𝑛3log𝑛𝑛) , where 𝑛𝑛 is the number of 
nondominated points from the expensive evaluations. Then, the 
time complexity of the EHVI calculation was reduced to 𝑂𝑂(𝑛𝑛2) 
in the 2-D case and 𝑂𝑂(𝑛𝑛3) in the 3-D case by the new general 
computation scheme proposed in [21]. In recent years, the time 
complexities of EHVI calculation in both 2-D [15] and 3-D 
cases have improved to 𝑂𝑂(𝑛𝑛log𝑛𝑛) [47]. In addition, Couckuyt 
[8] et al. proposed an exact calculation method for EHVI in 
higher dimensions, but it did not give the exact time complexity 
and indicated that it is still unable to escape the curse of 
dimensionality. Additionally, in higher-dimensional cases, 
Yang [46] et al. developed an algorithm for 𝑑𝑑-objective 
problems with time complexity 𝑂𝑂(𝑛𝑛⌊𝑑𝑑/2⌋). It can be seen that 
the time complexity increases as the dimension of the objectives 
increases. In this context, the computational cost of higher-
dimensional problems is still great, even intractable. For this 
reason, the exact EHVI infill method can only be implemented 
in the SAEA for MOPs and not MaOPs. For example, in [30] 
and [49], EHVI was implemented as the infill criterion of the 
proposed MOEAs. In addition, EHVI was applied to solve some 
engineering optimization problems with a small number of 
objectives [34], [48]. 
Concerning the challenge of the exact calculation of the 
EHVI, the approximation method is more acceptable in high-
dimensional objective problems. In [12], Emmerich first 
recommended applying Monte Carlo sampling (MCS) to the 
EHVI calculation. Luo [29] et al. applied MCS for EHVI 
calculation and tested the method with some benchmark 
problems. Svenson and Santner [42] proposed a maximin 
fitness improvement function as the measurement for 
improving the solution and calculated the expected maximin 
fitness improvement to select points for infilling by MCS in 
higher dimensions as well. Abdolshah [1] et al. extended the 
EHVI to a situation in which the priority of a specific objective 
could be expressed, which was also calculated by the sampling 
method.

To address the issue of time-consuming EHVI calculations 
in high-dimensional-objective problems, a new approximate 
EHVI calculation method is proposed in this paper as the infill 
criterion for expensive MaOPs, and its efficiency is 
significantly improved by the importance sampling (IS) 
approach. Based on the framework of NSGA-III [9], the 
proposed MaOEA is named NSGAIII-EHVI. To allow the 
optimizer to select more potentially valuable individuals in 
evolution, a new uncertainty-assisted nondominated sorting and 
selection method is proposed to replace the common approach 
in NSGA-III. The main contributions of this work can be 
summarized as follows: 
1) An efficient EHVI calculation method for MaOPs with the 
assistance of IS is proposed in this paper. To reduce the 
number of sampling times for the EHVI calculation, the 
objective space of the population is normalized to a unified 
scale before infill. Additionally, IS is applied to the 
normalized objective space using a uniform distribution to 
replace the normal distribution from each individual. Using 
uniform samples obtained by IS has two advantages. The 
first advantage is that the uniform samples can be used to 
estimate the improvement in the hypervolume of the 
samples over the PF. The other is that the uniform samples 
can be used to calculate the EHVI on a unified scale. The 
proposed method requires only one round of sampling for 
EHVI calculation, which is dramatically less than that of 
the conventional MCS, which needs to sample based on 
every individual in every population. 
2) The proposed algorithm considers the positive effect of the 
predicted uncertainty from the kriging model on expensive 
MaOPs, for which a new nondominated sorting method is

B. Nondominated Sorting with Predicted Uncertainty 
In the proposed NSGAIII-EHVI, infilling is performed based 
on not only the response prediction but also its predicted 
uncertainty, namely, the MSE from the kriging model in (2). 
However, in the ordinary NSGA-III, the environmental 
selection does not consider the uncertainty of the data, as all the 
data are assumed to be evaluated by expensive functions. This 
hinders EHVI in making the most of its role. In NSGAIII-
EHVI, therefore, a new nondominated sorting method is 
designed and applied to address this issue. 
Before introducing the detailed process of the new method, 
an example of a SOP is shown in Fig. 1, where the kriging 
model is trained by four observed data values. The prediction 
of the kriging model can be considered a normal distribution 
with mean 𝑦𝑦ො(𝒙𝒙) and variance 𝑠𝑠̂2(𝒙𝒙) from (1) and (2), 
respectively. A and B are the two predicted points shown in Fig. 
1, representing two situations for the prediction: the predicted

C. Importance Sampling for EHVI Calculation 
Through 𝑡𝑡max generations of evolution, the individuals with 
potential large EHVI values are collected to form the population 
𝑃𝑃𝑡𝑡max. The next task is to estimate the EHVI values of the 
individuals in 𝑃𝑃𝑡𝑡max. According to the suggestion in [12], the 
EHVI can be estimated by the MCS based on a normal 
distribution. Considering the difference in the normal 
distributions for different individuals, sampling should be 
conducted repeatedly. In addition, the dominance relationship 
of the samples should be recalculated to estimate the EHVI. 
This is very time-consuming. This paper implements IS for the 
EHVI calculation, reducing the number of samplings to only 
one.

IV. EXPERIMENTAL STUDIES 
In this section, the proposed algorithm is thoroughly 
investigated by the test suites DTLZ [11] and WFG [19]. Six 
test problems, IDTLZ1 [24], IDTLZ2 [24], IDTLZ3 (MaF4 in 
[5]), and IWFG4-6 (WFG4-1-7-1 in [23]), with inverted PFs are 
added to these two test suites. The inverted generational 
distance (IGD) [57] and hypervolume (HV) [60] are used as the 
performance metrics in this paper. In the IGD calculation, 
10,000 reference points are uniformly generated on the true PF 
using the method in [9] [44]. In the HV calculation, the 
reference point is (1,1, ⋯,1). In addition, the Wilcoxon rank-
sum test is employed to compare the performance of the 
algorithm in the ablation experiments and comparison 
experiments. The symbols “+”, “−”, and “≈” indicate that the 
compared algorithm is significantly superior to, inferior to, and 
not significantly different from the proposed algorithm at a 
significance level of 0.05. The proposed algorithm is coded 
based on the MATLAB platform PlatEMO [44]. For each test 
function, the experiments are conducted thirty times 
independently. 
A. Experimental Setting 
The parameters of NSGAIII-EHVI are listed and set as 
shown in Table III. The expensive MaOPs with a small sample 
size are more consistent with practical engineering problems, as 
only limited computing resources are available. Additionally, 
this situation is more challenging for MaOEAs, as surrogate 
models generally have low accuracy for a small sample size. To 
test the performance of the algorithms in small samples, the 
number of initial sample sets evaluated with expensive 
functions is set to 20, and the maximum number of evaluations 
with expensive functions is set to 100. The parameters 𝑡𝑡max and 
𝛽𝛽 in the proposed algorithm are recommended to be set to 15 
and 0.3, respectively. Due to page limitations, the experiments 
and discussions regarding the effects of these two parameters 
are given in the supplementary material. The reference point 
(𝑢𝑢𝑢𝑢 in Table III) for hypervolume calculation is generally set to 
a slightly worse point than the nadir point (one in the proposed 
algorithm), so it is set to 1.2 in this work. A recent study [22] 
provides a method to specify the reference point according to 
the number of objectives and the partition in each dimension of 
the given problem. However, it does not make evident 
improvement in the performance of the proposed NSGAIII-
EHVI algorithm. The related experiments are shown in the 
supplementary material. In addition, the parameters of 
evolution are the same as in the original NSGA-III [9] and are 
listed in Table III. 
In the following experiments of this section, the effect of the

C. Effect of the Introduced Importance Sampling 
To investigate the effect of the applied IS on the performance 
and efficiency of the algorithm, the proposed algorithm with the 
MCS method suggested in [12] is developed and is named 
NSGAIII-EHVI3 as the compared algorithm. In these 
comparison experiments, the DTLZ test suite with 5 and 10 
objectives is used for comparison without loss of generality. 
The number of decision variables in all the problems is set to 
10. The experimental results are shown in Table V with metrics 
IGD, HV, and running time. 
For the IGD metric, the results of NSGAIII-EHVI3 on 
DTLZ5 and DTLZ6 are better than those of NSGAIII-EHVI, 
but this is not the case in the other problems. For the HV metric, 
the results of NSGAIII-EHVI3 are better than those of 
NSGAIII-EHVI on the whole. This indicates that the introduced 
IS method does not improve the accuracy of the proposed 
algorithm and even slightly worsens it. This is because of the 
variation in the contraction of the sampling area. MCS based on 
a normal distribution is conducted in an infinite area, while 
uniform sampling based on the IS is limited to the normalized 
area. Regarding the points near the bounds of the uniform 
distribution, the error of the EHVI estimation becomes large 
relative to the MCS method.

greatly affected by the number of objectives, but NSGAIII-

D. Comparison with Other Algorithms 
In this section, the proposed NSGAIII-EHVI algorithm is 
compared with other state-of-the-art MOEAs. The compared 
algorithms are divided into two classes. The first class is the 
MOEA assisted by an EGO strategy, including ParEGO [27], 
SMS-EGO [38], and EIM-EGO [54], where SMS-EGO is based 
on the hypervolume infill criterion and EIM-EGO is based on 
the EI matrix criterion. The second class contains state-of-the-
art MaOEAs, including CSEA [35], KTA2 [40], K-RVEA [7], 
and HSMEA [17]. All the compared algorithms are 
implemented in PlatEMO [44] except for HSMEA. The number 
of objectives is set to 3, 5, 8, and 10 for each problem. The 
number of decision variables for all the test functions is set to 
10 except for WFG2 and WFG3, with 8 and 10 objectives, 
respectively, for which the number of decision variables is set 
to 11. The IGD results on the DTLZ test suite and WFG test 
suite are shown in Table VI and Table VII, respectively, where 
the best results are bolded in each row and summed in the last 
row of the table with the results of the Wilcoxon test. Due to 
page limitations, the HV results are shown and discussed in the 
supplementary material. 
From the results of the DTLZ test suite in Table VI, it can be 
seen that the proposed NSGAIII-EHVI algorithm obtains 21 
best results among 40 test problems, followed by KTA2 with 
15 best results, KRVEA with 2 best results, and SMSEGO and 
HSMEA with only 1 best result. ParEGO, EIMEGO, and CSEA 
have no best results in the 40 test problems. Combining the 
results of the Wilcoxon test with respect to all the problems, it 
is concluded that the performance of the proposed algorithm is 
significantly better than those of the compared algorithms 
except for KTA2 on the DTLZ test suite. 
For the DTLZ1 and DTLZ3 problems, KTA2 provides the 
best results among all the algorithms. This can be explained by 
the fact that in DTLZ1 and DTLZ3, a multimodel function is 
introduced, resulting in numerous local Pareto-optimal 
solutions to the problems and hindering the convergence of the 
solution. Because EHVI considers both the convergence and 
uncertainty of the prediction, NSGAIII-EHVI performs more 
poorly than KTA2, as KTA2 emphasizes convergence more. 
Compared with the other methods except for KTA2, NSGAIII-
EHVI presents slightly better results on DTLZ1 and DTLZ3. 
DTLZ2 and DTLZ4 are problems with a relatively simple 
fitness landscape and a spherical PF. NSGAIII-EHVI presents 
superior performance to the other methods on these problems 
but only on DTLZ2 with 5 and 8 decision variables, where 
KTA2 is better in terms of mean value. This indicates that the 
proposed NSGAIII-EHVI algorithm has the ability to obtain 
solutions with a good distribution. 
The PFs in problems DTLZ5-7 are irregular; the PF is a 
degenerate curve in DTLZ5, a set of disconnected regions in 
DTLZ6, and a combination of a straight line and a hyperplane 
in DTLZ7. The results in Table VI indicate that NSGAIII-EHVI 
performs better than the other methods on DTLZ6 and DTLZ7 
but is worse on DTLZ5. The reason is most likely that it is 
difficult for NSGAIII-EHVI to converge to the PF with a 
curved shape in DTLZ5, as only a few reference points in 
NSGA-III are distributed near the curve. However, for DTLZ6 
and DTLZ7, although the PF is irregular, the reference points 
in NSGA-III can cover most of the region. In this condition, 
NSGAIII-EHVI can use its ability to discover potential optimal 
points. Overall, the results indicate the feasibility of NSGAIII-
EHVI for problems with irregular PFs. 
IDTLZ1-IDTLZ3 are three problems with inverted PFs. In 
these experiments, the proposed NSGAIII-EHVI obtains 7 best 
results, followed by KTA2 with 5 best results. The other 
algorithms yield no best results. This indicates that the proposed 
algorithm has competitive performance in the problems with 
inverted PFs in the DTLZ test suite and is appropriate for 
addressing these problems. 
The WFG test suite includes problems that exhibit properties 
that are not evident in the DTLZ test suite [20], such as being 
nonseparable and deceptive. This suggests that the WFG test 
suite can provide more challenges for MOEAs. The results in 
Table VII clearly show that NSGAIII-EHVI is significantly 
better than all other compared MOEAs on the WFG test suite, 
which is indicated not only by the best results but also by the 
Wilcoxon test. NSGAIII-EHVI obtains 36 best results out of 48 
problems, followed by KTA2, with only 10 best results, and 
HSMEA, with only 2 best results. The Wilcoxon test results 
show that NSGAIII-EHVI is significantly better than KTA2 on 
nearly half of the problems but worse on only 4 problems. 
Regarding the other compared algorithms, the priority of 
NSGAIII-EHVI is more dominant. 
WFG1 and WFG3 are the only two problems on which 
KTA2 provides competitive results relative to NSGAIII-EHVI.

For WFG1, a number of transformation functions are 
introduced, making it difficult for MOEAs to maintain the 
diversity of the solutions [33]. Although NSGAIII-EHVI 
considers the diversity of the solutions, the emphasis is weaker 
than that of KTA2, which makes diversity one of the infill

NSGAIII-EHVI performs better than KTA2 on WFG2 but

results on DTLZ6 (with a disconnected PF), which show that 
NSGAIII-EHVI performs better than the others. This finding 
suggests that, compared to KTA2, NSGAIII-EHVI is more 
applicable to a complex problem with a disconnected PF. 
Discussion: From the results on both the DTLZ and WFG test 
suites, it can be concluded that the proposed NSGAIII-EHVI 
algorithm shows superiority to and more comprehensive 
applicability than other MOEAs. Relative to MOEAs with the 
EGO strategy, including ParEGO, SMSEGO, and EIMEGO, 
the performance of NSGAIII-EHVI is better, which can be 
explained by two factors. The first is the superiority of the 
optimizer. The optimizers applied in ParEGO, SMSEGO, and 
EIMEGO are not designed for MaOPs. Hence, the evolution of 
the population in these algorithms considers less diverse 
solutions, resulting in the obtained candidate solutions for infill 
having poor diversity. The second factor is the superiority of 
the infill criterion. The EI criterion used in ParEGO and 
EIMEGO is calculated for the SOP, which is somewhat crude 
for direct application to MaOPs. Although SMSEGO selects 
points based on the hypervolume, the use of the predicted 
uncertainty from the surrogate model is poorer than that of 
EHVI. In contrast, EHVI can obtain more comprehensive 
information on the improvement of the solution over the current 
PF, as it is calculated directly in a high-dimensional objective 
space. In addition, the three compared algorithms do not 
consider the diversity of the points for infill. In this context, the 
solution obtained cannot be distributed well on the true PF. 
Comparing the results for three state-of-the-art expensive 
MaOPs with those of KTA2, CSEA, KRVEA, and HSMEA, the 
proposed algorithm still performs better. Because these 
methods are all designed for MaOPs, the main reason for this 
involves the difference in the infill criterion. CSEA and 
KRVEA have the same infill strategy, which is based on 
reference vectors as in the method of HSMEA. The infill 
strategies of these three algorithms take no account of the 
predicted uncertainty. They give enough confidence to the 
predictions of the surrogate model to select desirable points 
with good convergence and good diversity. This can lead to the 
selected points deviating greatly from the prediction and failing 
to yield the expected ideal solution. Although KTA2 designs an 
uncertainty requirement to select the points with the largest 
uncertainty, it does not embed this uncertainty into the 
mechanism for convergence, resulting in only the improvement 
of the surrogate model accuracy and not the improvement of the 
infill performance. However, the EHVI can consider the effect 
of the uncertainty predicted from the kriging surrogate model 
on the selection of the new point.

VI. CONCLUSION 
To address the challenge of applying the EHVI to expensive 
MaOPs, this paper proposes the NSGAIII-EHVI algorithm 
based on the NSGA-III framework. Focusing on the infill 
criterion, the traditional calculation of the EHVI based on MCS 
is improved by the IS method, which achieves a significant 
increase in the computational efficacy of EHVI calculation with 
only one sampling. To use the EHVI to select candidate infill 
points 
with 
better 
quality, 
an 
uncertainty-assisted 
nondominated sorting method is proposed. In this method, the 
uncertainty is considered a dimension of the objective space for 
sorting, and the population is divided into a dominated set and 
a nondominated set, thus deploying different sorting methods. 
In addition, the infill criterion of the proposed algorithm 
considers the diversity of the solutions, thereby obtaining a 
more reasonable distribution of the solutions. 
The performance of NSGAIII-EHVI was tested on DTLZ 
and WFG test suites. The experimental results show that the 
application of the IS can reduce the computational cost 
significantly with little loss of accuracy and illustrate that the 
uncertainty-assisted nondominated sorting method proposed in 
NSGAIII-EHVI outperforms the traditional method. The 
experimental results also demonstrate the superiority of 
NSGAIII-EHVI in small-sample problems compared to other 
algorithms and show the great potential of the EHVI method for 
expensive MaOPs. This work fully utilizes the positive effect 
of the prediction uncertainty on the convergence of the solution 
but does not consider the uncertainty of the diversity. Therefore, 
future work will focus on the diversity of the solutions based on 
uncertainty.

Yong/NSGAIII-EHVI or from the PlatEMO [44].
